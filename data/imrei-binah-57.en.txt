The pledge I made in several of my previous chapters, to set out some fitting remarks concerning our holy tongue and the letters, vowel points and accents that belong to it,
I shall now take care to fulfil; may the good God answer me with a voice and extend His kindness to me.
However, to give the reader some respite between the topics, I shall devote a separate chapter to each of them.
Let this, then, be my discussion of the language. I say: even if there is one among our early sages, as will be explained shortly, who thought that all seventy languages existed even before the generation of the Dispersion,
the prevailing view, exalted in the mouth of all the upright, is that they came into being only in the days of Peleg, by the will of God,
and that until that time there was in the world only the holy tongue.
See their words, of blessed memory, in Bereshit Rabbah 18 and 31 on the verse "for from man (ish) was she (ishah) taken" [Gen 2:23]: "From here [we learn] that the world was created in the holy tongue.
Have you ever heard gini–ginia, antropi–antropia, gavra–gavreta? Rather ish and ishah. Why? Because this word fits that word." End of quotation.

It is true that the sage author of the Akedah, Gate 8, near the section on the union, wrote about them as follows: ⚠1
"You see that this is vanity, for who told us that it was not translated from another language," etc.,
and he drew their words toward an interpretation he found fitting; see there.
But in any case, apart from the consideration just mentioned, we have another strong and accepted [proof]: we see that throughout the ages and the narratives it is always said, Adam from adamah,
Havah from "mother of all living (hai)", Cain from "kaniti", Seth from "shat li",
Peleg "for in his days [the earth] was divided (niflegah)". And although those narratives were translated into all the languages of the nations, our eyes see that the names themselves were not changed,
but were carried over among all the peoples in the holy tongue itself: Adam, Cain, Noah and the like.
Is this not powerful evidence and faithful testimony from all the nations that these names were originally given in the holy tongue?

Take in hand this further proof as well, intelligent reader, which Rabbi Judah Moscato taught me:
it is fitting that the perfect be given to the perfect, especially by the Perfect One, the Creator.
For just as the natural philosophers said in explaining the sphericity of the heavens
that the most perfect shape was the one suited to that most perfect matter, as explained in chapter 52 of the second [book] of On the Heavens,
so we shall be right in saying that the Creator, blessed be He, who left nothing fitting lacking in His world,
did not refrain from bestowing upon the first Adam, who was perfect of his kind (as the Kuzari explained, Discourse 1), this language, which is perfect by virtue of several special properties it possesses;
in particular, it excels in signifying the natures and qualities of the things named, as the name nesher [eagle], which indicates the strength of its sight, from "ashurennu" [I behold him],
and also the shedding (neshirah) of its feathers when it renews itself, as is known; and the name yad [hand], whose numerical value equals the number of its joints, and its shared meanings;
and the other properties ascribed to it in the introduction to Sefer ha-Tikkunim and in the Sixty-Nine Tikkunim.

And as for its names for Him, blessed be He, it is already known that among the masters of the truth its special properties are multiplied a thousandfold, ⚠2
which has not been made known of any other of the languages of the nations of the earth.
Likewise its letters were exalted to signify the true nature of every being, lowly or high, as all this is explained by Nahmanides on the verse "and whatever the man called it," etc. [Gen 2:19],
both with respect to the language and with respect to the letters, which were without doubt created together with it, as will be further explained in the next chapter.
So too in the modes of signification which the Greeks and Romans call metaphora you will find splendid wisdom,
as in Jacob's words when he was preparing to die, "and I will lie down with my fathers" [Gen 47:30];
for in these two words are contained two great principles, on the truth of which many sages and scribes have wearied themselves writing books, and still their words are not sufficient.
For calling death "lying down" surely indicates the resurrection of the dead, their awakening and their rising, as is the way of anyone who lies down, who presumably will rise, unless because of the curse "now that he lies down he will rise no more" [Ps 41:9].
And had I been there with the sages of Perek Helek, I too would have answered my part: from here there is a hint of the resurrection of the dead from the Torah.

And his saying "with my fathers," to which the expression "gathered to his people" is also equivalent, surely indicates the survival of the soul,
for otherwise, where are his fathers and his people? I have already told you in the tenth chapter that the first of the sages of India, according to the version of Plutarch and Clement, when asked by Alexander, "Which do you think are more numerous, the living or the dead?"
answered, "The living," since he said that the dead are no longer anything.
Also the saying "and his soul is bound up with his soul" [Gen 44:30] truly encompasses all that the sages have set forth in their books on the true nature of love between two persons; and such things, highly regarded by the sages,
do we not learn them from the plain speech of the holy tongue?
Thus Rashi, of blessed memory, well explained "one language" [Gen 11:1] as the holy tongue;
and you will see that the numerical value of the one equals that of the other when the word itself is added,
as the sage of the Kuzari spoke well on this subject in Discourse 2, section 34, and Discourse 4, section 33.

Among the Christians, too, the great sage Augustine, in [The City of] God, book 16, chapters 3 and 11, wrote that from Adam to the Dispersion the whole world was of one language,
and when the lawless men of that generation sinned with a tongue speaking great things, saying "Come, let us go down," etc., they were likewise smitten in the tongue; ⚠3
but Eber and his household, who did not lend a hand to the scoffers, remained in their original language;
and not the whole house of Eber, but only his seed that continued to Abraham;
nor the whole house of Abraham, but only his chosen seed, Isaac;
nor all of Isaac, but only his seed, the house of Jacob;
for it was brought about by God that the holy tongue should remain in the holy seed. End of quotation.
These words of his incline toward what the Guide wrote in Part 1, where he spoke of the chosen people being the choice seed.

The author of the Akedah also touched on it in many of his gates, and so did Yedidyah [Philo] at the end of his tenth chapter on the verse "and Noah awoke" [Gen 9:24].
Take them from their place and your soul will bless me. It is true that Augustine himself, and also Annius, the commentator on the second book of Berosus the Chaldean, when speaking of the number of languages, said that there were seventy-two,
for they believe that this is the number of the sons of Shem, Ham and Japheth, of whom it is written "each according to his language" [Gen 10:5],
and Annius mocked one of their commentators who followed the view of our sages in the total of seventy, from the verse "He set the borders of the peoples," etc. [Deut 32:8].
Also Tostado, the great commentator among them, wrote on the section of the Dispersion as follows:
"Scripture does not make clear into how many languages they were divided; nor do we know whether in the course of time some language was lost with the destruction of its people in war,
or whether another language came into being by human agreement;
but since we find that the sons of Noah, of whom it is said 'each according to his language,' number seventy-two,
it is agreed by all that the languages were thus seventy-two." End of his words.
This difference between them and us is, in my opinion, that they included Asshur and the Philistines in their count,
which we do not, according to what I saw in the Pesikta de-Rav Kahana, found together with the midrashim Abkir and Abba Gurion in an ancient book I saw in the study house of the noble Isaac of Fano, resident of Ferrara;
for in a section on the Festival of Booths they are enumerated there one by one: ⚠4
fourteen for Japheth, thirty for Ham, twenty-six for Shem, making a total of seventy;
and among the sons of Ham it omitted Asshur and the Philistines, as we mentioned. This appears to be correct, since these two issued from others who had already been counted.

In any case, we shall not depart from the view of our sages, who agreed that the languages, the nations and their [heavenly] princes are seventy,
as you will find especially in Jonathan ben Uzziel on the verse "And the Lord scattered them from there" [Gen 11:8], which he renders: "And the Word of the Lord was revealed against the city, and with Him seventy angels corresponding to the seventy nations, each one with the language of its people
and the record of its script in his hand, and He scattered them from there," etc. And words like these are also found in chapter 24 of Pirkei de-Rabbi Eliezer:
"Seventy angels for the seventy nations, each nation with its script and its language," etc.;
and in Bamidbar Rabbah, section 13: "A basin weighing seventy [shekels], corresponding to the seventy nations, which extend from one end of the world to the other."
As for the aforementioned Berosus, although he did not attribute the confusion of languages to God, since he did not know the Name,
and wrote at the beginning of his fourth book that when Noah saw that mankind had begun to multiply,
he divided Asia, Africa and Europe among his descendants and sent each man and his household to a land assigned to him,
and that in the year 131 after the Flood Nimrod founded the city of Babylon and the wondrous tower, but could not complete them;
nevertheless, near the end of the third [book] he wrote that Noah was also called Janus, which derives from yayin [wine],
and that his wife, who was called Tytea, was also called Aretia, from erets [earth], since she was the mother of all the inhabitants of the earth,
and that after her death she was called Ashha, from esh [fire], for she ascended to the height of the elements, to the heavens, ⚠5
and other such things, which indicate the use of the holy tongue in those generations.

And in Cato, printed in the second part of the books of Berosus, you will find that the very ancient city of Arezzo in Tuscany was named after the aforementioned Aretia.
Likewise an Italian author, in a book he called The Origin of the Tuscan Language, ⚠6
after proving that that region is ancient and that its people first came forth from Noah,
cited nearly a hundred words found in that language that derive from the holy tongue,
such as besorah – ambasceria, mesurah – misura, harbonei kayits – carboni,
and the like. And one of their scholars, anonymous, in the introduction to a very fine Aramaic grammar,
which, from the words of the Bahur in the introduction to the Meturgeman, is evidently stolen from him,
wrote that at the time of the Dispersion many words of the holy tongue remained scattered and corrupted in most of the new languages,
and that those who were located near the place of the Dispersion retained a language close to the holy tongue,
such as Aramaic and Arabic and the peoples associated with them in the far East,
while the further they moved away, like Germany and the other regions of the West, the further their languages diverged from it.
This is close to what the sage Efodi wrote at the end of chapter 3, as follows:

"There is no doubt that when the nations agreed upon the seventy languages, they made use of some nouns and verbs from the language to which they had formerly been accustomed."
Also Rabbi David Provenzali, who stands as a banner of Torah in the city of Mantua, among his learned works
set down in a book he called Dor Haflagah more than two thousand Hebrew words scattered, some in Latin and some in Greek, Italian and others,
for example, in Latin, from ezer kenegdo uxor, and from atsilei yedei [Ex 24:11] asili, ⚠7
and from pilegesh, Greek and Latin pellex; ospedale from osef dalim [gathering of the poor]; Calliope, who is first among their nine Muses, the patroness of sweet song —
the Roman Arukh wrote that this name is derived from "beautiful voice," like the name kalinda, which he said derives from kol [voice], as we wrote at the end of chapter 40 of the third part. ⚠8
And academia is compounded from the expression beit eked ha-ro'im [2 Kgs 10:12], which Jonathan rendered "house of assembly of the shepherds,"
and from adam, meaning a gathering of people — as I replied concerning a society of scholars recently established here in Ferrara, which the first preacher described as an academia, and a certain speaker said that he found it strange that a Greek term should be applied to an assembly of Jews;
and when I told him that perhaps its origin is from Hebrew, as mentioned, his spirit was appeased and it pleased him.

From these things, then, besides the earlier proofs, we derive strong evidence that the holy tongue is ancient and the father of all other languages,
since its imprint is distinctly recognizable and has remained in each of them,
so that you would rightly judge it an advantage for those who possess it that they can understand from within it many words in the other languages of which one would truly say, "I hear a language I do not know" [Ps 81:6].
And it is falsehood that you will find in their great poet Dante, Paradiso canto 26, who put into the mouth of the first Adam the words:
"The language that I spoke was entirely extinct before the building of the tower was completed,"
for in fact it has remained, and blessed be the Lord, it is in our hands. On the contrary, as appears from the holy mouth of R. Simeon bar Yohai at the end of the portion Noah, all [the others] will perish and it will endure;
for after saying that the confusion of languages brought disputes into the world, as Ibn Ezra says in the narrative of the Dispersion on "and speaking according to the language of his people" [Esth 1:22],
he concluded: "But in the time to come, what is written? 'For then I will turn to the peoples a pure language,' etc." [Zeph 3:9].
There is a hint of this: the letters of safah berurah [a pure language] are numerically equal to those of bi-leshon ha-kodesh [in the holy tongue],
except for one, which, though it is the material of all numbers, is, as you know, not itself a number.

Also concerning the great Christian sage Aquinas I have heard that in one of his works he said that the holy tongue alone will remain when the Lord restores His world to His will as in days of old.
However, you will find in the Jerusalem Talmud, Megillah chapter 1: "'And the whole earth was of one language,' etc. R. Eleazar and R. Yohanan: one said that they spoke seventy languages, and the other said that they spoke the language of the Unique One of the world, the holy tongue."
Similarly in Bereshit Rabbah, section 38: "'And of one speech (devarim ahadim)' — united speech: what this one had, that one had, and what that one had, this one had;
and the Rabbis say: one language." End of quotation. For with this refined word of God, namely the verse "And the whole earth," etc., it happened not only as the Master, the Guide, said in Part 2, chapter 29, about the word avah, which for the Hebrew means "he was willing" and for the Arab "he refused,"
but also as he wrote at the beginning of his Treatise on Resurrection, that two people of one and the same language may understand the same text in plainly contradictory ways.
So with this narrative of the Dispersion: it is possible that one of the aforementioned pair of our sages, seeing that even before the building of the tower it is written of the descendants of each of Noah's three sons "each according to his language, by their families, by their languages" [Gen 10:5, 20, 31],
and perhaps also finding it difficult to reconcile how, when they were dispersed in a moment, they learned their languages so quickly as to speak of the many things needed,
thought it right to stretch and interpret the verses in a manner that would make everything come out right,
and said that the meaning of "one language" is that they all spoke in every manner alike,
so that although the words were many, they were likewise known to all,
and that with the little loosening that the attribute of judgment cast among them they separated from one another,
and each man and his household held to a particular language for all the days of the earth.

Nevertheless, as I said, the view that prevailed above and was agreed to be true among our ancients is the view of the other [sage] mentioned, that they all spoke the holy tongue,
and so I have seen in the Targum of Jonathan ben Uzziel and the Jerusalem Targum on the verse "And the whole earth was": "And the whole earth was of one language and one speech and one counsel, in the holy tongue in which the world was created from the beginning."
And I know of no one, even among the sages of the nations, who took it into his heart to confound the correct plain meaning of the narrative of the Dispersion,
except Yedidyah the Alexandrian, who in my view, as I wrote in the sixth of these chapters, is like a koy among those who speak [of these matters], and a creature unto himself in relation to our sages.
For in his book On the Confusion of Tongues he confounded his words, and all but said that the narrative only alludes to the confusion of false opinions engendered by material notions rising up against the Lord of all the earth, as I mentioned about him in chapter 5,
though at the end of that chapter he somewhat saved himself by saying that one who believes the words literally has lost nothing,
yet at the same time he considers the divine words to be like bodies, and their hidden treasures to be at the level of souls within them.
Now with wisdom and understanding the divine Kuzari wrote, in the places mentioned, that this holy tongue did not come into being through a people's agreement on its individual words,
but was formed and made habitual on [Adam's] tongue from the time he became a living soul — as Onkelos has it, "a speaking spirit" [Gen 2:7];
for immediately upon his creation, when he was like one twenty years old, as they said, of blessed memory, in Bereshit Rabbah 14, Bamidbar Rabbah 12, and Shir Rabbah on the verse "Go forth and behold" [Song 3:11],
even while he was alone, he had to hear the voice of God speaking to him about the tree of life and the tree of knowledge.
And such a voice (as [the author of] the Kuzari wrote, Discourse 1, section 47, and Discourse 2, section 2, concerning the voices at the giving of the Torah) is not as the philosophers thought of prophecy, that it comes from a soul whose thoughts are purified and cleave to the Active Intellect, etc.,
but by the will of God the air reaching the prophet's ear takes on the forms of the letters signifying the matters He wishes to make heard,
as the sage Recanati also wrote on the verse "And Abraham rose early in the morning" [Gen 22:3], on the verse "And all the people saw [the voices]" [Ex 20:15].

Yedidyah the Alexandrian also fixed this firmly in his book entitled On the Ten Commandments,
and there is support for their words in Shir Rabbah on the verse "Let him kiss me" [Song 1:2], where they said that the utterance itself was engraved of its own accord and its voice was heard;
therefore Adam needed an understanding of the language, as we said.
But as for the other languages, we come back to the dispute of the Amoraim above: if they preceded the generation of the Dispersion, they may have been conventional, by human choice;
and if they were bestowed at that time and engendered according to their families, they too may be described as ordained by God —
not as a new creation, but as an awakening of learning, like the spirit that stirred in individuals among them to invent the crafts, as is known.
This is what we mentioned above in the name of Jonathan ben Uzziel and Pirkei de-Rabbi Eliezer: that the angels, that is, the agents of providence, taught each nation its language.
And I was pleased to see that the ancient Christian Origen, too, in his eleventh homily on the book of Numbers, wrote as follows:

"Different angels busied themselves with the language and speech of different nations; as if to say that one angel placed the Babylonian language in one man and another the Egyptian language in another, and so with all of them;
and thus the language given to Adam from the beginning remained in that seed which is not the portion of any angel or prince, but the portion of God."
Now once it is established that this chosen language was the inheritance of the congregation of Jacob alone,
one must know that, as the Kuzari says in Discourse 2, from the time of our father Abraham he and his sons had two languages:
Hebrew, appropriated to his seed as the holy tongue, and Aramaic as the profane tongue,
especially since they were sojourners and residents in those lands which, although many and differing from one another in elegance of speech —
as if to say that the dialect of the Chaldeans within Aramaic was on the level of the dialect of the people of Meshech in Italy — ⚠9
nonetheless that whole region, or most of it, spoke the language of Aram,
as the aforementioned anonymous author wrote in his introduction to the aforementioned Aramaic grammar, as follows:

"The Aramaic language is used not in the lands of Babylonia alone, but also in Mesopotamia (which is Aram Naharaim), Assyria, the cities of Arabia and elsewhere."
So too you can easily understand from the Roman Arukh, under Syria, that this name, which among them signifies Aram, comprises countless lands.
And our forefathers, who gave names to cities and places — Adonai Yir'eh,
Rehovot, Gilead, and Mizpah — and the names of the tribes and of the sons of Joseph and of Moses,
did so on the basis of the language appropriated to them; likewise the names of the patriarchs themselves, especially Abraham and Sarah, are surely of the coinage of the language used by the holy spirit.
As for the name Moses, notwithstanding all that Ibn Ezra wrote about it on the portion Shemot,
Yedidyah the Alexandrian, who undoubtedly knew the Egyptian language, wrote at the beginning of the book The Life of Moses that the Egyptians call water mos;
and accordingly the explanation "for I drew him out of the water" [Ex 2:10] is very fitting, both from the Egyptian side, on account of the water, and from the Hebrew side, on account of his being drawn out of it.

However, after Israel entered the land in the days of Joshua — when those born in the wilderness had already been purified of the defilements of the Egyptians and of their stammering tongue through their long stay there, for so, in my opinion, the wisdom of His providence decreed —
together with what they said, of blessed memory, in Pirkei de-Rabbi Eliezer at the end of chapter 48, in the Mekhilta on Bo el Par'oh, section 5, in Tanhuma, portion Balak, and in Shir ha-Shirim Rabbah on the verse "A garden locked" [Song 4:12],
that even in Egypt they did not change their language, it is credible that all of them, even the common people, always spoke the holy tongue.
Here are several faithful witnesses: "Say now shibbolet, and he said sibbolet" [Judg 12:6];
and the wordplay of "the jawbone of an ass" on hamor hamoratayim [Judg 15:16];
and many names of persons and places, such as Gilgal, Jerubbaal, Ramath Lehi, Naomi, Samuel, Jedidiah;
and likewise Obed, according to the words of Josephus, Antiquities, book 5, chapter 11, that the neighbours alluded in it to his serving (ya'avod) Naomi in her old age, following their saying "a son is born to Naomi" [Ruth 4:17].
And many others besides these, which, although the narratives of their affairs were translated into other languages,
the names themselves were not changed from what they were originally given.
And Hezekiah's men said to Rabshakeh: "Speak, we pray, to your servants in Aramaic, for we understand it" [2 Kgs 18:26] — although it was foreign in our land —
"and do not speak with us in Judean," which is the holy tongue, "in the hearing of all the people," etc.
And I have seen, among several ancient coins belonging to David Finzi, a resident of Mantua, a silver coin
on one side of which is the image of a man's head, and around it is written "King Solomon" in the holy tongue and in square script,
and on its other side the image of a temple, and around it is written "Temple of Solomon."

And if the book of Eldad ha-Dani, who came from the Ten Tribes, deserves some credence, notwithstanding all that Ibn Ezra wrote about it on the portion Shemot,
it states clearly that they and also the Sons of Moses speak the holy tongue,
for on foreign soil they preserved the language they had formerly used in their land,
as is also found written in chapter 3 of the Fourth [Book of] Ezra, which is accepted by the Christians and the Greeks, ⚠10
that after they were removed into exile they resolved to return to the Lord and no longer to intermingle with any of the nations of the earth that had caused them to sin;
for this reason they chose to move still further away from the place to which they had been exiled.
Likewise what is related in the book Darkhei No'am by Ibn Habib about the poem in the holy tongue engraved on the tombstone of King Amaziah's army commander, as we shall write in the chapter on poetry,
is strong support for this, and we learn several things from it, as you will see there.

And Jeremiah's words "Thus shall you say to them," etc. [Jer 10:11] were, contrary to his custom, in Aramaic,
since he wished them to be heard by the nations themselves, the inhabitants of Babylon, as if to say: tell them these words of mine in the language they understand.
It is also well known that at the beginning of the Babylonian exile Nebuchadnezzar ordered those youths to be taught the script and language of the Chaldeans [Dan 1:4], which is Aramaic, implying that until then they did not know it;
and Daniel had no need to relate to us the words of the Chaldeans to the king in their own language,
except to show that, among the other kindnesses of the Lord toward him, he had learned it thoroughly,
all the more so as his [Aramaic] is the most elegant of its kind, as is easy to discern when we compare the Aramaic of Daniel with that of Ezra or anything else we possess.
However, during the seventy years of exile, all the masses of Israel who settled in the various lands of the nations undoubtedly changed their language,
and in particular learned the Aramaic of the province of Babylon and its environs,
so that when the distress of exile inclined the hearts of the children to turn away from the iniquities of their fathers — who in the days of the First Temple had neglected and forgotten the Torah and its commandments,
so that concerning the circuit of Jehoshaphat's officers to teach in the cities of Judah it is written, "and they had with them the book of the Torah of the Lord, and they taught among the people" [2 Chr 17:9],
and Hilkiah the priest in the days of Josiah said, as one announcing news to the multitude, "I have found the book of the Torah" [2 Kgs 22:8],
and it is an explicit verse, "and you have forgotten the Torah of your God" [Hos 4:6] — they had in their exile neither books nor means to study it except in the Aramaic language known to them, translated and interpreted as best they could,
and with many variations, as some of the learned know, whether in words or in some matters, as happens to anything popular and to anything poured from vessel to vessel.

And when the Lord again had compassion on them, Ezra the scribe was roused and restored the Torah, refined and purified, in its Assyrian script and its holy tongue,
which, as the Kuzari says in Discourse 3, section 15, had been preserved in the hearts of the priests and the Sanhedrin because of the need to render just judgments;
but the common people, and especially those living in the outlying districts, knew and understood only the version customary among them in Aramaic, as in former days.
Is it not clear at the end of the book of Nehemiah that, owing to the scant Judaism preserved among many of them,
some were treading winepresses on the Sabbath and loading asses [Neh 13:15],
and their children spoke half in Ashdodite and could not speak Judean, but only the language of each people [Neh 13:24]?
And so Aramaic speech continued even among the people of Jerusalem throughout the days of the Temple;
for Hillel the Elder, a hundred years before the Destruction, said in the vernacular, "He who makes worldly use of the crown," etc., and "Because you drowned," etc.
Also Josephus, Antiquities chapter 8, book 3, speaking of the priests and their garments, said:

"In our language (that is, in the Aramaic then in use among them) they are called kahanei,
kahana rabba, mikhnesin kehuna," etc., as I mentioned in chapter 9 of part 1 and cited his very words in chapter 49 of this fourth part.
And Samuel the Small (b. Sanhedrin chapter 1 and Sotah, chapter Eglah), he too said at the time of his death: "Simeon and Ishmael to the sword, and his sons to slaughter, and many troubles," etc.;
so much so that when a heavenly voice was granted to certain pious men concerning secular matters,
the words were Aramaic, as mentioned in the Jerusalem Talmud, Sotah, chapter Eglah, in our Gemara, chapter Elu ne'emarin, and Megillat Ta'anit chapter 11:
Yohanan the High Priest heard a voice from the Holy of Holies saying:
"The young men who went to wage war at Antioch have been victorious"; and again, the incident of Simeon the Just, who heard a voice from the Holy of Holies:
"Annulled is the work that the enemy said [to bring]," etc. And I have already written in the fifth of these chapters concerning Yedidyah the Alexandrian, and in the ninth concerning the translation of the Torah,
things pertinent to this subject, especially the words of the Christian apostles in Aramaic; it would be fitting for the reader to take them from there without further ado, for they are essential to this matter. ⚠11

And from the currency of Aramaic among them at that time you will find that the Christians' translator, in his preface to the book of Judith and to the book of Tobit, which are accepted by them but not by us,
wrote that he translated them from the Aramaic language in which they were composed, since they and their events were after the Babylonian exile;
not so the book of Baruch, since he was a disciple of Jeremiah while the Temple stood.
And the book "The Great Wisdom of Solomon," called the Book of Wisdom, about which Nahmanides said in his introduction to the commentary on the Torah:
"I have seen the translated book," etc. — all of whose words you will find in chapter 7 of it —
I think it likely, if it is not a translation, that it was composed by King Solomon in Aramaic in order to send it to some king at the far end of the East.
Ezra the priest, however, laid his hand only on the books composed by the prophets through the holy spirit and in the holy tongue,
and our sages, in their understanding and insight, upheld only those confirmed and established by him.

It also appears that even after the Destruction that custom continued in the remnant left in Jerusalem and its environs, as in the language of the books of the Zohar of R. Simeon bar Yohai and his companions;
and R. Yohanan composed the Jerusalem Talmud in light Aramaic, as they say at the beginning of Bava Kamma;
and in Megillah chapter 1, R. Yohanan said, "I remember when I was a child," etc.; and the language of the popular documents, even in the Mishnah and baraita:
"Female children," "male children," "from the depth of the abyss," etc.,
as mentioned by the author of Sefer Keritut, part Leshon Limmudim, gate 2, section 36.
But in this connection one should not mention the Aramaic found in our [Babylonian] Gemara and in some of our prayers,
for it is from the language of the sages of Babylonia and the orders established there,
as the Tosafists wrote in the name of R. Isaac, Berakhot chapter 1, concerning the Kaddish, that there were ignorant people there who knew only Aramaic, which was their language.

However, around the time of the Second Destruction, the good Lord, whose mercies are not exhausted, so that His holy Torah, in its script and language, should not be forgotten from the mouth of our seed,
touched the heart of every wise-hearted one among the Tannaim, and especially our holy Rabbi, and they transmitted to us, among the other traditions, the holy tongue and the holy script on its proper basis,
both in the Torah, Prophets and Writings (which in any case, as Hillel the Elder told the proselyte in Shabbat chapter 2, we received from them),
and in the mishnayot, baraitot and toseftot, all of which are consistent and "the tongue of the righteous is choice silver" [Prov 10:20].
Often, too, the examination of a halakhah is set straight when we notice that the anonymous Talmud introduces some Aramaic words among the words of the Tannaim we mentioned.
And perhaps to strengthen this intention they also dissuaded the multitude, with regard to public prayer — even though it is among those things that may be said in any language —
from arranging it (especially that corresponding to the daily offering) except in the glorious language in which the Men of the Great Assembly arranged it, and not in Aramaic,
as they said in Berakhot and in chapter Elu ne'emarin: "Let a person not request his needs in Aramaic, for the ministering angels do not attend to it";
and they also said that they do not understand it — this by way of figure of speech, as the Tosafists say in the aforementioned chapter Elu ne'emarin —
to indicate that it is unseemly in their eyes to help us by it to bring our prayers into the holy sanctuary.

All this in order to observe and ensure that whatever is called by the name of the Lord in public, whether Torah or prayer,
should no longer be in the corrupt Aramaic version customary among them of old,
but that the Torah should return, as far as possible, to its own precise lodging.
And perhaps for this reason, in my view, was what is related in the Jerusalem Talmud, Shabbat, chapter Kol kitvei, and tractate Soferim chapter 15:
"It happened that Rabban Gamaliel was standing on the building on the Temple Mount, and they brought him the book of Job written in targum, and he said to the builder: Hide it under the course of stones."
By reasonable conjecture it was one of the popular versions that did not correspond to what is found in Holy Scripture.
But so that the multitude should not cease to understand the words of the Torah as far as they could, they maintained the interpreter in some places,
and likewise they ordained (Berakhot chapter 1) the completion of the weekly portions with the Targum of Onkelos, refined sevenfold;
and you already know the words of the Semag and his fellows at the end of positive commandment 19, that according to some, [Rashi's] commentary is suitable for us in place of it.

But let us return again to the holy tongue and say that it is fitting to mention concerning it something found in the Jerusalem Talmud, Megillah chapter 1, and likewise in Midrash Esther at the end of section 3, namely:
"Four languages are fitting for the world to use: la'az for song,
Romi for battle, Sursi for lament, Hebrew for speech." End of quotation. The meaning of la'az for song is the Greek vernacular,
since you should know that Greek has one language reserved for writing and another in which a man speaks to his fellow,
concerning which they said, of blessed memory, in Megillah: "The Greek vernacular is valid for everything."
And I have found our sages using sometimes the one and sometimes the other: for example, asimon in chapter Ha-Zahav, which is silver [coin];
moros, a fool, in the Pesikta on "Hear now, you rebels (ha-morim)" [Num 20:10] — this is vernacular;
and from it, in my view, sifrei ha-miram at the end of Yadayim and Hullin chapter 3, that is, books of folly.

Also diyateki, meaning a covenant, that is, a deed of conditions; and therefore their saying in Bava Metsi'a chapter 1, "da tehe le-mekam" [this shall stand], is only an Aramaic wordplay on the Greek,
as with the word apotiki, of which they said "apo tehe kai" [on this it shall stand]; and kapendaria: "rather than go around the rows, I will enter through this";
and the like, which are wholly Greek, yet they set up a marker for them in Aramaic as well.
But hydor, which we mentioned in the chapter on Aquila, signifying water,
and ariston, a meal, belong to the written [language].
Romi is without doubt Latin, and the meaning of "for battle" (le-kerav) is to preach in public, as we say in Sukkah and in Shir Rabbah, end of the verse "Who is this coming up" [Song 3:6]:
"What is 'the powders of the merchant'? One who reads and repeats [Scripture and Mishnah], a battler (karov) and a poet."
And Sursi is evident from the Mekhilta chapter 3 and Pesahim chapter Tamid to be Aramaic,
for R. Josiah said: "'Ye shall make your count (tavosu) for the lamb' [Ex 12:4] — this is the Sursi language, as a man says to his fellow, 'Slaughter (kos) this lamb for me'";
and chapter Merubbah: "The Sursi language was given honour in the Torah, 'Yegar Sahaduta' [Gen 31:47], and in the Prophets, 'Thus shall you say to them,'" etc.

And Rashi at the end of tractate Sotah wrote as follows: "The Sursi language is close to Aramaic,
and I say that this is the language of the Jerusalem Talmud, and the Ishmaelites call it Suriano." End of his words.
And perhaps Rabbenu Tam was right, in chapter Merubbah, in saying that it is the elegant and refined Aramaic of the regions of Syria.
The word ilya is Greek, signifying a voice of weak crying and lamentation; so you will find in Arakhin chapter 1: "an ilya fell into their mouths,"
and Rashi explained: a lament, as the Targum renders "on the bare heights" [Jer 3:21] as "lament on the hilltop, ilya." ⚠12
And last but most beloved, the holy tongue, which is beautiful for speech; for what goodness and beauty could be greater than its being truly natural to the first Adam when he came forth from the womb of his mother, the earth,
and likewise to his descendants, the fruit of his loins, until sin brought about [its loss] in the days of Peleg?
And how could anything rise above its rank, since we find the voice of the living God speaking in it all the words of the Torah and prophecy, and its being particular to the holy people and the holy land?
Therefore the words of Nahmanides, of blessed memory, at the beginning of the portion Ki Tissa, are correct,
and those of Efodi at the end of his book, with a company of mighty grammarians,
who said that for this reason our Rabbis called it the holy tongue;
nor are the words of the Master, the Guide, in Part 3, chapter 8, false, that it has this name because it has no nouns and verbs primarily denoting the shameful organs.

In my eyes, what Ibn Ezra wrote on Isaiah 19, "On that day there shall be five cities in the land of Egypt speaking the language of Canaan" [Isa 19:18], is strange:
"From this we learn that the Canaanites spoke the holy tongue";
and likewise Nahmanides on the verse "that it is my mouth that speaks to you" [Gen 45:12], who said that this was the language of Canaan.
For Isaiah in this prophecy came to allude, as they said, of blessed memory, at the end of Menahot, to the altar of Onias, which was erected with Ptolemy's permission in the city of Alexandria in Egypt, as we have also mentioned in some of these chapters;
and therefore he said that because so many people of the Land of Israel would go there,
the inhabitants of those cities would learn the language of Israel dwelling in Canaan,
that is, the Aramaic language used among the Jews at that time of the Second Temple, as we mentioned.
And so Rashi explained there as follows: "The language of Canaan — the language of Israel who are in the land of Canaan,"
for this is why it did not say simply "the language of Israel," but their language at that time in the land of Canaan.

And the verse "that it is my mouth that speaks to you," which Nahmanides brought as proof,
they, of blessed memory, already used in Tanhuma at the end of Balak, Vayikra Rabbah 32 and Shir Rabbah on the verse "A garden locked" to prove that our forefathers did not change their holy tongue despite all their dispersion hither and thither.
Therefore its meaning is as Rashi said: "My mouth that speaks to you — in the holy tongue."
And I, as a precious novelty, add to the words of the aforementioned sages a sound reason for the attribution of the epithet "holy" to our language:
namely, that since it was a divine creation in the first Adam from the days of Creation, as we mentioned,
and not agreed upon by human beings like the other languages, even though divine assistance accompanied them,
they are all destined for perishing, like all human works, as we wrote in this chapter on the basis of the Zohar at the end of the portion Noah,
while it alone will endure together with the other created things of which it is said, "And God saw all that He had made, and behold, it was very good" [Gen 1:31].
Thus the epithet "holy," signifying enduring and eternal, is very apt for it, as they said, of blessed memory, in Sanhedrin chapter 1 on the verse "he shall be called holy" [Isa 4:3]: "Just as the Holy One endures forever, so the righteous endure forever."

And know that, since "all the ways of a man are pure in his own eyes" [Prov 16:2] and a place finds favour with its inhabitants,
Galen, the Greek physician, in the first five chapters of his second book On the Differences of Pulses,
made bold to write that Greek surpasses all other languages in beauty, not only in its breadth and abundance but also in its pronunciation,
all of which, compared to it, resemble the lowing of cattle or the chirping of birds and creeping things.
But Rabbi Maimonides, of blessed memory, who as far as possible raises up our bowed state,
at the end of the Aphorisms of Moses on medicine, took him to task for this, in addition to striking him as his wickedness deserved for speaking, in his eleventh book On the Usefulness of the Parts, chapter 14, against the servant of the Lord, Moses, concerning the eyebrows.
And from the disparagement of the aforementioned Galen we have learned the praise and the righteous humility of the Rabbi, of blessed memory, who, for all his burning anger against him for these sins,
never ceased to call him the man perfect in his craft, namely the craft of medicine, of which that book of his speaks,
but only said that he found in him the kind of malady whereby one who sees himself perfect in one branch of knowledge imagines himself also perfect and an authority in all others.

In any case, we have a future and great hope for our holy tongue from the good promise which the mouth of the Lord spoke concerning the time when we shall go forth from darkness to great light:
"For then I will turn to the peoples a pure language, that they may all call upon the name of the Lord, to serve Him with one consent" [Zeph 3:9]. Amen, may He do so speedily in our days.
