Now that we have grown old [and turned] to [the lessons of] schoolchildren: since we are near the end of this work of ours, [we turn] to something of a novelty in itself, and also to learn from it a few things contained in these chapters of ours,
we shall begin to study the shape of the letters. Not in our own script, but in the one which [the Sages] of blessed memory, in chapter 1 of Megillah and chapter 2 of Sanhedrin, called the Hebrew script,
that is, [the script] of the people of 'Ever ha-Nahar, which they also designated the Libona'ah script,
and Rashi explained [it as] large letters, like those written in amulets and mezuzot, which is in use among the Kutim, that is, the Samaritans.
Maimonides of blessed memory, too, in his commentary on Mishnah Yadayim chapter 4, wrote of it that it is the [script] in use among the people called al-Sāmira, that is, Shomron.
Now, according to what I saw in a booklet by a trustworthy man from the Land of Israel, which he wrote in his own hand for Rabbi Petaḥiah Yedidiah of Spoleto while teaching him the Arabic language as well, and which Rabbi Moses the physician, his son, showed me here in Ferrara,
and also according to what, afterwards, in the city of Mantua, the learned Rabbi Samuel Marli showed me in the booklet of the journey of the eminent Rabbi Moses Basola of blessed memory to the Holy Land and the novelties he saw on that journey, in the very handwriting of the said eminent [rabbi],
and likewise according to what the learned Rabbi Reuben of Perugia showed me there in Mantua, in a version given to him by one of the Christian scholars in the city of Bologna, as he found it in an ancient book held reliable among them,
this Samaritan alphabet has this form. And these are the words of the said Rabbi Moses Basola:
"The letters of the Kutim which are on the coins." Now you will find that Naḥmanides of blessed memory wrote, after [his] commentary on the Torah, as follows:

"The Lord has blessed me thus far, that I came to Acre and found there, in the hands of the elders of the land, a silver coin engraved with the engravings of a seal;
on one side something like an almond rod, and on the other side something like a flask, and on both sides, all around, writing engraved very clearly.
And they showed the writing to the Kutim, and they read it at once, for it is the Hebrew script that remained with the Kutim, as is mentioned in Sanhedrin.
And they read on one side: 'Shekel of shekels,' and on the other side:
'Jerusalem the holy.' And they say that the images are Aaron's rod with almonds and blossoms, and the second image the jar of manna.
And we weighed it at the money-changers' tables, and its weight was ten silver [pieces], which are half an ounce, as Rashi mentioned.
I also saw [a specimen] of that coin, with those images and that writing, of half its weight, and with it they would weigh the half-shekel that they used to weigh for the offerings," etc.
And indeed, blessed be the Lord, I the writer have also seen one of the aforementioned shekels, in the possession of the widow of the pious merchant Isaac ha-Agiv Kafri of blessed memory, who was a resident of Ferrara and, in the prime of his good name, went to Jerusalem and died there, and was buried in the Valley of Jehoshaphat outside the Zion Gate, about a mile away. ⚠1
And this woman, on whom children depended, returned to the honored master Yom Tov of blessed memory, her eldest son, who is in charge of his father's property here in Ferrara. ⚠2

And around the said coin I saw written, in the letters above: "Shekel of Israel,"
and in its middle a jar with [the letters] shin dalet over it, which in my opinion is an abbreviation for "Shekel David," ⚠3
and on the other side: "Jerusalem the holy" or "Yerushalayimah qedoshah," and in its middle a rod with three blossoms, according to these images of its two sides:
And my heart tells me that Rabbi [Moses] ben Naḥman of blessed memory forgot, and wrote "Shekel of shekels" instead of "Shekel of Israel."
Likewise, though it is possible to read "Jerusalem the holy," it is also possible to read "Yerushalayimah qedoshah,"
for so it is found in the Jerusalem Talmud, Megillah chapter 1: "Rabbi Simon and Rabbi Samuel bar Naḥmani both say: The people of Jerusalem used to write Yerushalayim [and] Yerushalaymah and were not particular; and likewise Ṣafon, Ṣafonah; Teiman, Teimanah."
And from this you will understand what they said in the Jerusalem Talmud, Yevamot chapter 1, on the mishnah "Beit Shammai permit the co-wives to the brothers," etc.: "Rabbi Simon said: The reason of Beit Shammai is 'The wife of the dead man shall not be [married] outside (ha-ḥuṣah) to a stranger' [Deut 25:5]:
the outer one (ha-ḥiṣonah) shall not be [married] to a stranger. Beit Shammai turn out to be like these Kutim, who perform levirate marriage with the betrothed and release the married, for they expound ḥuṣah [as] ḥiṣonah.
How do Beit Shammai uphold 'and has no son'? Rabbi Jacob of the South said before Rabbi Yose: 'And has no son' — from the married one;
the outer one shall not be [married] to a stranger. Rabbi Yose said to him: May the Kutim not 'bless' you, for you uphold their exegesis.

Rabbi Simeon ben Eleazar taught: I said to the scribes of the Kutim: What caused you to err?
[It is] that you do not expound like Rabbi Neḥemiah, who taught: Any word that requires a lamed at its beginning and was not given [one],
give it a he at its end, as in la-ḥuṣ — ḥuṣah;
le-Se'ir — Se'irah; le-Sukkot — Sukkotah. They objected to Rabbi Neḥemiah: 'The wicked shall return to She'olah' [Ps 9:18]!
[Answer:] to the lowest storey of She'ol" — end [of quotation]. And this conclusion is also in Midrash Tehillim on the said verse "The wicked shall return."
The upshot is that the Kutim do not admit that a he at the end of a word stands in place of a lamed at its beginning,
and therefore they interpret ha-ḥuṣah as ha-ḥiṣonah; and so Beit Shammai appear to expound as they do.
And Rabbi Yose said to the said Rabbi Jacob, "Al yeḥasdunakh Kuta'ei," that is, may the Kutim not curse you,
for so I found written in the said notebook from the Land of Israel, that when they wish to say a curse upon him they say "ḥisda upon him."
And perhaps "lest he who hears it put you to shame (yeḥassedkha)" [Prov 25:10] is from this sense, apart from what Naḥmanides wrote on it at the end of Parashat Qedoshim.

Likewise from the shape of these letters you will understand a fundamental saying in the Jerusalem Talmud, Megillah chapter 1, namely: "Rabbi Levi said: According to the one who says the Torah was given in Ra'aṣ, the 'ayin was a miraculous work;
according to the one who says the Torah was given in Ashuri, the samekh was a miraculous work."
For in the Tosefta, Sanhedrin chapter 4, and in Megillah chapter 1 and Sanhedrin chapter 2 in our Gemara, and Megillah chapter 1 in the Jerusalem [Talmud], the tannaim disputed the letters in which the Torah was given at Sinai, as we shall also mention in chapter 58:
Rabbi Yose and Rabbi Nathan said that it was this script of 'Ever ha-Nahar,
and Rabbi Simeon ben Eleazar, Rabbi Eleazar ben Perata, Rabbi Eliezer ha-Moda'i and Rabbi, ⚠4
according to whom the generality [of opinion] holds, said that it was the Ashurit script.
And so Rabbi Levi said that according to the one who says the Torah was given in Ashuri, the samekh was a miraculous work; and the allusion is to Rav Ḥisda's saying in our Gemara: "The mem and samekh in the Tablets stood by a miracle,"
since their view, of blessed memory, was that the meaning of "on the one side and on the other they were written" [Exod 32:15] is that the letters passed through from side to side.
And on this view what they said is well settled: "Why is it called Ashuri? Because it is me'ushar (straight/praised) in its letters," as the Babylonian [Talmud] says,
or "me'ushar in its script," as the Tosefta and the Jerusalem [Talmud] say, that is, praised and splendid, as Maimonides of blessed memory explained on Mishnah Yadayim chapter 4.

And according to the one who says [it was given] in Ra'aṣ (which is necessarily the aforementioned script of 'Ever ha-Nahar), the 'ayin was a miraculous work,
for you have seen it as a complete enclosure, like the shape of our samekh.
And with this view the other reason they gave there accords: "Why is it called Ashuri? Because it came up with them from Assyria,"
just as they also said in the Jerusalem [Talmud] of Rosh ha-Shanah that the names of the angels and of the months came up with them from Babylonia.
As for what you see in the Jerusalem [Talmud], Megillah chapter 1: "Ashuri has a script but has no language;
Hebrew has a language but has no script; they chose for themselves the Ashuri script and the Hebrew language" —
there is no doubt that by "has no" they mean that it is not praiseworthy,
as the Tosafists wrote in chapter 1 of Avodah Zarah, s.v. she-ein lahem lo ketav ve-lo lashon, as follows:

"We say in Megillah: 'And I will cut off from Babylon name and remnant' [Isa 14:22] — 'name' is script, 'remnant' is language.
But what does it mean, seeing that they speak the Aramaic language and still use it?
Rather it certainly means the language of royalty" — end of their words. Now you will find that the author of the Arukh explained the word ra'aṣ as inverted, like be-ṣa'ar (in distress),
or we read be-ra'aṣ in the sense of crushing and pressure, which is likewise a matter of distress.
But in any case it is evident that the meaning of be-ra'aṣ is the script of 'Ever ha-Nahar.
And their saying in chapter 2 of Sanhedrin, "it was turned for them into ro'eṣ," which according to Rashi's explanation is from the sense of "[Your right hand] shatters (tir'aṣ) the enemy" [Exod 15:6],
means that it is the opposite of the Ashuri, whose supporters are made happy (ye'ushshar),
or that the word ra'aṣ is from the Samaritan language and indicates in some way the shape of that script. ⚠5

And there is no doubt that the inclination of Rabbi Joseph Albo, Discourse 3, chapter 16 of his Sefer ha-Ikkarim, ⚠6
toward [the view] that the Torah was given in the script of 'Ever ha-Nahar is naught and void,
while the view of the Sages, which we shall mention further in chapter 58, that it was given in Ashuri, shall be remembered for good forever.
— And on this matter I will not refrain from informing you that Rabbi Moses Alashkar wrote in his Responsa, no. 74, as follows:
"Know that there came into my hands some of those coins, of several different kinds, shekel and half-shekel;
and some of them have written on them 'Year such-and-such of the consolation of Zion,'
'Year such-and-such of King so-and-so'; and I saw on one the image of a bound lulav with an etrog beside it.
And a Jew expert in that script told me that on one side it was written in Greek, with the arms of the Greeks engraved on it,
and on the other side written in Hebrew; and it seems this was in the time of their subjection to the Greeks," etc.; see there.

And the shape of the letters that the author of Sefer Miqneh Avram brought at its beginning — it is certain that they are not the letters on the above-mentioned shekel.
Further on this matter, let me remind you that Rashi, in explaining the verse "thirty shekels of silver" [Exod 21:32], said as follows:
"The shekel weighs four gold pieces (zehuvim), which are half an ounce" — although at the beginning of Parashat Ki Tissa he wrote zuzim in place of zehuvim.
And Philo of Alexandria, in his first book of The Special Laws, on the commandment of valuations, wrote as follows:
"The shekel is four silver pieces." And Josephus in the Antiquities, chapter 9, book 3, wrote as follows: ⚠7
"The shekel of the Jews is four Attic drachmas." And in my opinion the "sixty thousand darkemonim" of Ezra 2 and the "thousand adarkonim" of Ezra 8 are [the same as] the aforementioned drachmas,
and zehuvim, zuzim, silver pieces, darkemonim and adarkonim were all one thing.
And the living man who sets his heart to know precisely all the weights, biblical and rabbinic,
and likewise all the measures of their kinds, dry and liquid, let him seek out the book Kaftor va-Feraḥ, chapter 16, for it examined and explained them in great detail according to all the views found.
