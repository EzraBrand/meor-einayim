Now Aristeas, in the course of what he says about Eleazar the high priest, wrote as follows:
"Is he not awe-inspiring in the greatness of his splendor, which derives from the glory of the robe he wore and the precious stones upon it?
For from this garment, which reached down to his feet, there hung golden bells that gave forth a pleasant sound, and between them pomegranates adorned with flowers and various colors.
And he had a girdle, etc., and on his breast he bore, etc. And on his head there was a covering which they call a sudar, and upon this sudar there was a mitra, made with wondrous workmanship, which is a most splendid ornament among kings.
And from it extended a beaten plate of gold upon which was the name of God, etc.," as is written in [the section] Hadrat Zekenim above. ⚠1
And you, the discerning reader, ought to consider these words of his just as they are.
And if it seems difficult to you why he did not mention the tunic, the sash, and the breeches, the answer is at hand: he wrote only what was visible to the eye, and could not see or write about what was beneath his garments.
Note that he mentioned the beautiful girdle, which is the band of the ephod, and likewise he mentioned the breastpiece, which is plainly visible.

The robe and the ephod, however, he reckoned as a single garment, since together they appear to the eye as one garment reaching from the parts of the neck to the feet.
And this is among the things indicating that the ephod did not descend from the belt downward, as Rashi and his circle of companions say,
but only from the shoulders to the girdle, behind him, as appears from the words of the sage Don Isaac [Abravanel], whom we have mentioned,
and that it was doubled over in front of him to join the breastpiece, as is known.
Likewise it becomes clear from his words that the bells were not inside the hollow of the pomegranates, as Nahmanides and Don Isaac say,
but rather as all the other commentators say: between every two pomegranates a bell, and between every two bells a pomegranate.
It has also been made clear from his language that the turban (mitznefet placed on the head of the high priest is what is called a sudar,
which we find in Curtius, Book 3, where he mentions the ornaments of that Darius who fell into the hands of Alexander, ⚠2
and among the Persians it was called cidaris, that is, sudar in the language of our Rabbis. It was an ornament of distinction among them [the Rabbis] too, of blessed memory, as appears in [tractate] Shabbat, chapter ha-Motzi Yayin: "sudar — 'the secret of the Lord is with those who fear Him' [Ps 25:14]" [b. Shabbat 77b], ⚠3
and especially for one who was married, as appears in the first chapter of Kiddushin: "he saw that he did not spread a sudar over his head" [b. Kiddushin 29b].
From this word paris ["spread"] it appears that one need not say that it encircled [the head] as a wrapping, but only that it covered the head,
as they say at the beginning of the chapter Benei ha-Ir, "this perisa is an accessory, etc." [b. Megillah 26b], according to both explanations of the author of the Arukh, s.v. pras.
And the Jerusalem Targum [renders] "and he put a veil over his face" [Exod 34:33] as sudara. Accordingly the term migba'at, which we shall discuss further in a later chapter, suits the sudar of the high priest.
Likewise it is revealed from Aristeas's words that upon this sudar was a mitra, upon which the golden plate was set on the side of the forehead.
One should know that this word mitra, among those versed in the Latin language, most often denotes a pointed hat, slightly curved at its top, which was customary in Egypt on the heads of men and women,
and sometimes also denotes merely a band, a kind of diadem that does not cover the crown of the head.

Now in both of these senses it corresponds exactly to the term kippah mentioned by our Rabbis when they say, "A woolen kippah was on the head of the high priest, etc." [b. Zevahim 19a],
for the author of the Arukh, s.v. kippah, according to his first explanation, explained that an unqualified kippah is a kind of hat that also covers the crown of the head;
and according to his second explanation he follows the words of Alfasi, chapter Ba-Meh Ishah, who, since it seemed to him impossible that the thread (petil of blue mentioned with the frontlet (tzitz should be wider than the frontlet itself, which is two fingerbreadths,
said that the kippah mentioned by our Rabbis in this connection, and likewise the one mentioned among a woman's ornaments with respect to the Sabbath, is nothing but a cord of braided work two fingerbreadths wide, as we mentioned above.
Take it to heart, discerning reader, that the ambiguity of the word petil has misled many commentators,
because, believing that it denotes only a single thread, they strove to interpret the petil mentioned with the frontlet according to that meaning, and could not fit it with the term kippah that our Rabbis mention in this connection.
It is true that sometimes it denotes specifically a thread, as in "and he cut [it into] threads" [Exod 39:3],
but most often it denotes an ornament or a cord, and anything small or large made of many threads woven together,
or that which is twisted around some object — around the head, say — just as a single thread too is called petil because it is itself twisted.

See Kimhi under the root ptl, who wrote as follows:
"'Tzamid patil' [Num 19:15] means a small piece of cloth, and similar to it in the Mishnah is 'the petilah of a garment';
and likewise, in my opinion, 'your signet and your patil' [Gen 38:18], meaning his mantle or the wrap on his head; and this is also the view of the Targum, [which renders] shoshifakh;
and the Targum [renders] 'and he wrapped his face in his mantle' [1 Kgs 19:13] be-shoshifeih, and the Targum [renders] 'the shawls' [Isa 3:22] shoshifa, and the Targum [renders] 'and they shall spread the cloth' [Deut 22:17] shoshifa." End of quotation.
And Rashi too explained petilekha as "your garment with which you cover yourself." End.
And this is on account of the garment itself, woven of threads in network fashion or of twisted work,
not because it was a fringed garment (tallit metzuyetzet, as Nahmanides and Mizrahi, of blessed memory, smear [him] there with whitewash. ⚠4
And Nahmanides himself wrote there as follows: "Perhaps petilekha is a small sudar with which one sometimes wraps the head, and it is called petil because it is narrow like a thread."

And Ibn Ezra, on the commandment of tzitzit, mixed his pepper, as is his way, to wage war by his stratagems; see there. ⚠5
And the author of the book Mikhlal Yofi, who collects the words of the choicest commentators, wrote on Judah's petilekha as follows: ⚠6
"It appears that the garment or the wrap was made in the form of two or more, for that is why it says 'and the cords' [Gen 38:25]." End of quotation.
But according to our approach we say that the reason is that it was made of many threads visible in it as distinct from one another, in latticework and network fashion or twisted work, as we mentioned.
And so on the basis of Aristeas we can understand that the petil mentioned with the frontlet means not specifically a thread,
but a kippah, as they [the Rabbis], of blessed memory, say — in whichever of its two explanations it may be;
concerning which, with the Creator's help, we shall speak further in the chapter on Josippon, which is chapter 49 below.
However, the terms nezer [diadem] and tzitz [frontlet] are not explained at all from his words,
for he did not mention, regarding that wondrous workmanship, what designs and floral openings (petur tzitzim were in it,
by which we could judge that these terms befit it.
Yet this too, alongside that, will be made clear with the Creator's help in the aforementioned chapter 49.
