Know that in the translation ascribed in these generations to the seventy elders, of all the thirteen changes mentioned [by the Sages] only four are found, and these are they:
"And He finished on the sixth [day] and rested on the seventh" [cf. Gen 2:2]; "and he set them upon a carrier of men" [cf. Exod 4:20];
"which they and their fathers dwelt in the land of Egypt and Canaan" [cf. Exod 12:40]; "I have not taken one desirable thing of theirs" [cf. Num 16:15].
But if you look for further divergences, or additions and omissions, in other places, whether in the Torah or in the Prophets and Writings, you will find them beyond counting.
For the variations of vowels, letters and words are very many, and the gravest of all is the shifting of the verse-division, so that you find the end of one verse made the beginning of the next, as when you read: "and cause him to be beaten before him according to his wickedness,
by number. Forty he shall strike him" [Deut 25:2–3]; and in the Prayer of Moses: "and from everlasting to everlasting You are God. Do not (al, with pataḥ) turn man to dust" [Ps 90:2–3].
This occurs very often, in such a way that the meaning is corrupted by the confusion.
For this reason some translators among the Christians who have arisen in our generations have placed signs and numbers on the verses, so that they should not overstep their bounds relative to what we have in the Holy Scriptures.

And according to their text the nun-verse is said in Ashrei: "The Lord is faithful in all His words and upright in all His deeds" [cf. Ps 145:13].
Perhaps they understood it as referring to the future, according to the tradition of R. Ezra which the sage Recanati of blessed memory mentioned in his commentary on the verse "And Abraham rose early in the morning" [Gen 22:3], that it is destined to be said in the days of the redemption, for then indeed these words will be fitting.
What is stranger to me is that I went through all forty-three books of Yedidyah the Alexandrian [Philo], printed together;
and he lived before the destruction of the Second [Temple], one of the inhabitants of Alexandria, the very city of the translation, as we mentioned in the previous chapter.
Yet in more than fifty verses he adopts (as I complained of him above) the altered text found in the translation ascribed to the Seventy and interprets them in his own manner, as if that were how it is written before God in heaven.
Likewise Josephus, who was a worthy Jew, and who himself said at the beginning of his history that although in his time there were four sects of Jews—
Sadducees, Boethusians, Gablanim, and Pharisees—he cleaved in love to the sect of the Pharisees, who are our Sages;
although in the course of his narratives he does not happen to mention Holy Scripture except rarely, you will find at the beginning of the twenty books of the Antiquities which he wrote for the Romans that, when he mentions the years of the ten generations from Adam to Noah,
he follows the path of the aforementioned translation and raises the total of 1656 years to more than two thousand.

Against this you should also know and understand that, apart from the translation of the Seventy, many translations were made after it in those early generations by Greek-speaking Jews.
The witness to this is the translator for the Christians [Jerome] in his preface to the Chronicle of the Caesarean [Eusebius], who wrote as follows:
"The books translated by the seventy elders do not have in the Greek language the sense proper to them;
therefore Aquila, Symmachus and Theodotion made other translations differing from it.
The one endeavored to translate word for word, the other attended rather to the sense, and the third did not depart much from the way of those elders.
As for the fifth, sixth and seventh translations, although their authors are not known,
they possess such merit that they too have been held in esteem." Thus far his words.
And were I not afraid, I would say that perhaps it is to these three elders that the author of Tractate Soferim, chapter 1, alluded when he said: "It happened that five elders wrote the Torah in Greek," etc.,
as I cited it at the beginning of chapter 7. For although there is a difference between five and three,
I have no doubt that it is an imprecise statement,
just as the whole of Tractate Soferim, according to the words of the Rosh of blessed memory in Hilkhot Sefer Torah, which we shall mention again, God willing, in chapter 19, is not as authoritative for us as the other books of our ancients.

All the more so since there is a difficulty here: if those five interpreted the Torah for Ptolemy, what further need had he for the seventy-two? And further, what is this they said, that the Torah could not be translated into Greek? On the contrary, it is said explicitly in the Yerushalmi, Megillah chapter 1, that it could not be translated adequately except into Greek.
Do not be surprised that the author of the said tractate places the translation of the five before the translation of the seventy-two, when he says "Again it happened," etc.,
for you find our Sages saying in the chapter Elu ne'emarim: "A heavenly voice went forth from the Holy of Holies in the days of Yoḥanan the High Priest,"
and then a similar incident in the days of Simeon the Just, although Simeon the Just, the first of our Sages after the Great Assembly, necessarily preceded the said Yoḥanan the High Priest by a long time;
rather, sometimes there is no earlier and later. And the said Aquila is Onkelos [Aqilas] the proselyte, whom we shall mention, God willing, in chapter 45, together with the two translators who followed him.
Moreover, after all the aforementioned translators, that Christian [Jerome] too arose and translated the whole Bible in the days of Damasus, the thirty-ninth pope, some twelve hundred years ago,
although I have not heard that this translation of his actually exists at present. ⚠1
And the Christian sage Augustine, who lived in the time of the said translator, used two versions, namely the one ascribed to the Seventy and another besides,
for many times you find him saying in his commentaries "another version."
And see him in book 18, chapter 43 of his City [of God], where he too mentions six translations, including the version of this said translator.

Now, after we have laid down as an established foundation, as reason would suggest, that the translation of the said seventy-two elders
was from the source of Israel, that is, from the Torah scroll which we have in our script and language, and that this is what they brought to King Ptolemy,
and that their interpretation was so fitting and correct in the eyes of all the Jewish sages who saw it on the day it was done, as Aristeas, Yedidyah and others who recount that affair related—
there is surely a great difficulty: what happened to it, or to us, that we now see it so different from the Scripture we have?
And why did the ancients need the further translations we mentioned? We shall not pass over making known that the aforementioned chief of the Christian sages [Augustine], in his City, book 15, chapter 13, took up this difficulty and divided his answer into two parts.
First he laid down as a firm foundation that the text we possess is correct and upright;
and then, concerning some of the changes, he said that the spirit which formerly appeared upon the prophets to write what they wrote
is the one that afterwards appeared upon the seventy translators to change what they changed,
since he believed that there is support in that change for what they [the Christians] believe,
so that in his opinion both these and those are to be considered words of God.

But regarding the number of years from Adam to the Flood, where he saw that this view of his was not tenable,
we must truly praise him, for he pleaded on behalf of us all and said: it is not to be believed that the Jews, a people scattered and dispersed, could have agreed upon falsifying the books they possess, which all accord in a single style;
and even granting that they could have done it, it is far-fetched to think that out of envy of strangers they falsified their own holy books;
likewise it cannot be said that those Seventy, who were chosen as sages and who were honored with all the well-known honor,
perverted their judgment so as to write things that have no sense.
Rather it is correct to judge that in the period close to the true translation, the Greeks and Egyptians who were then found in wickedness or folly falsified the numbers of years and some other passages in the first copy which they brought out,
and from it the leprosy spread to all the other books written like it.
This is the gist of his words there, and you will find further support from him in chapter 11 concerning a change found in the years of Methuselah.

Likewise in book 18, chapter 43, he said that after the Hebrew text, whose honor is preserved forever,
the translation of the Seventy is to be preferred above all others; and therefore also in chapter 44 he endeavored to reconcile a contradiction found in the matter of Jonah, "yet forty days" [Jonah 3:4], where they wrote "three days," as he saw fit there.
And he is consistent in his view concerning us, for in the eighth part of his works, in the commentary on Psalm 40, he calls us "the chest of books."
So too the sage Aquinas, who follows in his ways, described the Jews by this very epithet in his commentary on the Epistle to the Romans, chapter 9;
meaning by this that we, through the Masorah and the markers that we set up for ourselves in the Holy Scriptures, guard the books from all corruption and harm.
And this is one of the places that the one distinguished in imperial law, Alessandro da Imola, considered in his opinion written in the sixth part of his works, no. 233, to defend the Jews in whose books improper things had been found.
Thus according to them we are not to be blamed, nor are the said Seventy, for these changes.
In particular the sage Samotio, book 1, chapter 7, brought proofs that the number 1656 years is the true and correct one, ⚠2
from the fact that the chroniclers of very ancient times, such as Berosus the Chaldean and other writers on the origins of nations and kingdoms, all agree in their reckonings with our Torah;
and he draws support from Josephus, who at the beginning of his first book against Apion said that the agreement of differing writers on one matter is surely faithful testimony that it is free of falsehood.

And this premise conceived and bore for him [the conclusion] that the number of years from Adam to Noah found in the translation of the Seventy and the Greeks, which is not as in our Torah, is necessarily an error;
and that all the sages who held to their way—whether to please the nations of their time so as not to stir up controversies against them, like Josephus in many places and especially book 10, chapter 11,
or because, to preserve the custom of their fathers, they followed the system of the Greeks, like Eusebius, Clement of Alexandria,
Justin, Isidore and their like—all spoke falsely, for their count is vain.
And on this matter he cited there the words of the chief of the sages of their religion whom we mentioned,
and said that they are correct. Also the bishop Eugubinus [Agostino Steuco] in his Recognitio, on the portion Balak, on the verse "in the end of days" [Num 24:14], writes thus; look at him if you please. ⚠3
Likewise the translator for the Christians [Jerome]: if you go through his prefaces to Genesis, Samuel, Chronicles and Job, and his commentary on chapters 5 and 6 of Ezekiel, you will find that, among his other remarks on the translation of the Seventy which we mentioned in the previous chapter,
when he wishes to defend himself against those who mocked him for translating the Holy Scriptures,
saying that the elder sages, the well-known seventy elders, had already preceded him and that their translation is the authoritative one,
he first says that they were indeed sages but not prophets by whose mouth we all live.

And granting that they and their words were true, their translation itself does not exist in the world except mixed with several kinds of errors, both through the ancients who were close to the time it was made,
and through the copyists with the scribe's pen, for passing from hand to hand it has not remained free of mistakes and transgression.
And in particular in the preface to Chronicles he wrote that the Alexandrians follow the translation of one of their sages called Hesychius, and three other regions which he mentions there each follow the text of its own sage, differing one from another;
and that Origen mixed the words of the Seventy with the versions of Theodotion; and because of these confusions, the need of the hour and the entreaties of his friends compelled him to translate everything anew from the true source that we possess.
And he constantly urges anyone in doubt about the versions to inquire of the Jews as the guardians of their truth from of old.
So then, reader, your eyes may see plainly that he is at one with the said chief of the sages of their religion in thinking that the translation now found ascribed to the Seventy
is not the true one that issued from their hands, but hands have been laid upon it.
But as to the investigation of why this befell it,
it stands to reason, as their said chief sage wrote, to attribute one of the principal causes to the malice and the natural and deliberate wickedness of the Greeks who were then inhabitants of Alexandria and the other dwellers in the lands of Egypt,
and we may suspect them, saying that they laid hands on it so as to leave no root of its excellence.

For you will hear below faithful testimony that the Greeks in Alexandria at that time were by their very nature inventors of fictions and lies,
and how with all the inclination of their hearts they hated the Jews with utmost hatred from the days of their exodus from the land of Egypt;
and with all the honor that Alexander of Macedon and King Ptolemy showed our ancients, as is known,
it was heaping coals upon the heads of the Alexandrians, so that it is very likely that as soon as their [the kings'] shadow and dread was removed from them they set out to shame and disgrace them [the Jews], gouging out every right eye of theirs like Nahash the Ammonite [cf. 1 Sam 11:2] according to the view of our Rabbis.
Go and see what Yedidyah the Alexandrian wrote in his last works, and Josephus in his two books against Apion the Alexandrian:
that there was no animosity of nation toward nation in the world like that of the Alexandrians toward our people, and they always sought to heap insults upon them with all their might.
Therefore it is easy to judge that out of hatred and envy they did not rest or cease
until they falsified and corrupted that translation whose fame had gone forth among the nations as perfect in beauty.

Did they not do the same to the books of one of the great sages of the Christians, Origen by name, a citizen of Alexandria itself in the days of Pope Urban I? For even in his lifetime they laid hands on them and attributed to him opinions far from his own,
on account of which he himself uttered piercing words against them, as is seen in the seventh Apology of Giovanni Pico, Lord of Mirandola;
and in this connection he also mentioned there several other sages to whom the like happened, especially men of Alexandria.
Hearken now and hear what a great king, upon whose lips is divine judgment so that he does not err [cf. Prov 16:10], namely Caesar Augustus, said of them, ⚠4
for in his Commentaries, book 4, he wrote as follows: "If I came to defend the Alexandrians and to say that they are not deceitful and brazen, I would spend my words in vain,
for at all times, by their origin and nature, they are known to people as ready to commit treachery, and you will not find anyone of a contrary opinion." Thus far his words.
So that the words of the said Christian sage, who said that the Greeks and Egyptians laid hands on the translation of the Seventy, have very much to rely on,
and the other sage who answered Amen after him, as we mentioned, rightly pursued justice with good sense.

Further, besides all that has been said, one should know that the great house which Ptolemy established for the academy of Alexandria, and in which the translation of the Seventy itself was preserved,
was entirely burned when Caesar Augustus made war on [the city], when he pursued Pompey, who had fled there under the protection of Cleopatra.
The writer Aulus Gellius bears witness to this in book 6, chapter 17, saying: "There were more than seven hundred thousand books in the academy of Alexandria, ⚠5
and in the first war, when the city was breached, the soldiers burned that house, not by the king's order." Thus far.
And the writer Orosius confirmed the like in book 7, as did other writers of Roman history.
Accordingly, given these two causes of loss—either the wickedness of the gentiles who falsified the translation as stated,
or that the great fire we mentioned consumed it—it should not be loathsome in our eyes if several men among those Greeks set their hand to translate the holy Scriptures anew.
And you already know that according to Elijah Baḥur the Levite in the book Masoret ha-Masoret, and also according to the bishop, author of the Recognitio, in several places on the Pentateuch, at that time there were no vowel points or accents for the letters,
since in their opinion they did not yet exist. Or at least you may say, according to our view, which will be explained further in the fifty-ninth of these chapters, that by custom most of their books were without vowels and accents.
And so those translators who entered into the thick of the beam [i.e., undertook the difficult task] according to their bare reading, without the support of proper tradition,
may easily be thought to have stumbled and been caught in many errors, for want of one who reads correctly.
Let these words I have written in answer to this question be upon your heart until you hear from me in the next chapter another approach.

Be that as it may! It is precious in my eyes to strike the throat of the said falsifiers and to refute it from the opinion of the greatest of the Christian sages,
since there are those who accuse us, saying that we have dealt treacherously with our books, adding and subtracting.
And you, reader, with good sense and knowledge, and as servants speaking to their masters, say like R. Abbahu in Bereshit Rabbah, parashah 14: "From your own I bring you proof" —even unto our books; ⚠6
and the word of our God shall stand [Isa 40:8], holy forever, enduring, [down to] "the hooks of the pillars" [Exod 27:10], all that we have in the Holy Scriptures.
The Lord will not reckon it as iniquity to any of us that there is room to reflect upon some sayings falsely ascribed to our Sages, as you will see in chapter 19.
Today we have found him a man of understanding; and you, call out with your mouth so that your soul may bless me. ⚠7
Now from what we have mentioned we learn that the tradition of our Sages about the change of only thirteen items
is very possible, should you posit seeing the translation of the Seventy itself;
and there is no proof against them from the fact that we see the translation now ascribed to them abounding in changes beyond number, for this is not that.

There is also support for their words, of blessed memory, from what I mentioned in the previous chapter, that they completed their work in seventy-two days;
and in that short time it is not within human capacity to translate all twenty-four books properly; and they did no little in interpreting in it the Torah of Moses alone.
Therefore this [translation] comprising all the books is certainly not the one we sought.
Likewise the astonishment we had at the man Yedidyah and the like, how in their books they walked in the footsteps of the altered translation presently found ascribed to the Seventy, as we mentioned,
is nullified as negligible; for from the days of the said Alexandrian falsifiers, and also from the time of the said fire, to the time of Yedidyah,
many years passed, as is easily seen from the tables of Samotio, for from the Seventy to Yedidyah nearly three hundred years elapsed.
In the meantime, then, the other or others spread, as we wrote.
And remember, do not forget, what we pointed out at the end of chapter 4, that there is proof from the two books of the said Yedidyah
that the text of the Seventy was falsified by others in the number of years from Adam to Noah.

And if Yedidyah and Justin, in their places that we mentioned in the previous chapter, said that still in their time many of the inhabitants of the towns around Alexandria gathered every year to the island of Pharos, the dwelling-place of the translators, to celebrate and sacrifice as an eternal memorial, for they cherished its stones and favored its dust [cf. Ps 102:15],
this is no proof that their translation was then extant in its proper state;
for in any case, whether it then existed or not, that multitude already held and accepted that those noble men were godly, and believed that the land in which they dwelt was a camp of God, very awesome, and always ready to shed forth supernal abundance from it,
as in the words of the Kuzari, second discourse, that all three nations believe so about Jerusalem because of the Shekhinah that rested there.
And add to this that the leaders of Alexandria would naturally have loved the continuance of that custom,
for the profit which no doubt was cast into their purse in bundles of silver from the foreigners gathered there.
There is moreover a particular excuse for Yedidyah and Josephus, as we mentioned above: since the words of their books were addressed to the Greeks of their time, they saw that for the time being it was better not to change, because of the controversy;
similar to what I mentioned there from the words of the translator for the Christians [Jerome] about the total of seventy-five souls, where in that connection he also added and said
that likewise anyone of theirs who spoke to the nations adduced the verses as proof according to the text he knew to be current in those days.

And all these things are additional testimony to the truth that is in our books.
But now I wonder: for we find our Sages of blessed memory cherishing the Greek language, saying in the first chapter of Megillah that they permitted books to be written in no language except Greek, on the ground of "the beauty of Japheth shall dwell in the tents of Shem" [Gen 9:27; b. Megillah 9b];
and likewise in the Yerushalmi, Megillah chapter 1: they examined and found that the Torah cannot be translated adequately except into Greek;
and in the chapter Merubah Rabbi said: "Either the holy tongue or the Greek tongue";
so much so that in the Yerushalmi, Peah chapter 1, and Shabbat chapter Ba-meh ishah, R. Abbahu said in the name of R. Yoḥanan: a man is permitted to teach his daughter Greek because it is an ornament for her.
And we also find our Sages particularly praising the making of that translation, and it was for them an honor to Israel,
as is seen from the manner of their words there, as follows: "When they permitted Greek, they permitted it only for a Torah scroll, and because of the incident of King Ptolemy," etc.;
they also said that they were aided in it by God. If so, what is this found in the first chapter of Tractate Soferim: "It happened that five elders wrote the Torah in Greek for King Ptolemy, and that day was as grievous for Israel as the day on which the calf was made, for it could not be translated adequately";
and in the appendix to Megillat Ta'anit it is stated that one fasts on the eighth of Tevet, on which the Torah was written in Greek in the days of Ptolemy, and that darkness came upon the world for three days, as we mentioned at the beginning of chapter 7?
Indeed, besides the fact that at the beginning of our chapter we protested against this statement of Tractate Soferim, so that we might say "this baraita is corrupt,"
and moreover, if they said of the deed of the five elders that it was grievous as stated, they did not say so of the deed of the seventy-two—
it seems that in any case one may say one of two things: either that those sages chosen to go proclaimed a fast and solemn assembly for the whole congregation, as Esther did when going to the king,
and like the deed of Yedidyah the Alexandrian when he went to plead on behalf of his people before the emperor Gaius concerning the statue, as is seen from his forty-third book;
and this because darkness then covered the faces of the Jews out of fear lest their emissaries be put to shame, and the sin of our people;
and so for many years, until Megillat Ta'anit was abolished, some of them kept that custom, like "the matters of the fasts" of Esther "and their cry" [Esth 9:31] of old.

Or we may say that they feared lest the righteousness and kindness that Ptolemy did in this be, as our Sages of blessed memory say in Shir ha-Shirim Rabbah, a sin for Israel,
in the manner of what they also said in chapter 4 of Sanhedrin concerning "and he offered him as a burnt offering, and there was great wrath," etc. [2 Kgs 3:27]: "You have not acted even like the proper ones among them." ⚠8
For the true Judge saw, together with this, His near ones in some generations of the Second Temple being more remiss than those who are not commanded and yet do,
indeed they turned to heresy by forming factions of Sadducees and Boethusians, and therefore all the troubles came upon us,
and the proselytes of the gentile nations became as hard for us as a scab. And evidently God was pleased with the deed of Ptolemy, for the reign of the Ptolemies lasted long, up to twelve rulers, as is explained in detail in the tables of the Caesarean [Eusebius] and of Samotio.
For if the gentile Dama ben Netina, for the honor of his father, a mere mortal, merited all the wealth and honor that [the Sages] of blessed memory mentioned in the first chapter of Kiddushin, and as we shall further mention in chapter 38,
how much more will the Holy One, blessed be He, not withhold the reward of Ptolemy, who so honored His Temple, His Torah and His people in this affair.
What emerges for us, then, from this chapter according to its main intent is
that the statement of our Sages of blessed memory about the change of only thirteen items is possible,
and that from the words of the greatest of the Christian sages it has been made clear that, with all the versions found in the translation of the Holy Scriptures, and especially in the number of years of the world up to the Flood,
the truth and the correct [reading] is as written in our holy words.
