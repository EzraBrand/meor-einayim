Now, the Greek Xenophon composed two long books, recently translated into the Roman tongue [i.e., Latin or Italian]: ⚠1
one on Cyrus the First, who captured Babylon, and one on Cyrus the Second.
At the beginning of the book on Cyrus the First he wrote that this Cyrus was the son of Cambyses king of Persia, and that his mother's name was Mandane, daughter of Astyages king of Media. ⚠2
Over the course of eight books he speaks at length in his praise:
how he captured Babylon in a single night, while its king was drinking himself drunk on a festival day of the Babylonians,
and how he seized many other lands besides. At the end of the eighth book he wrote that the boundary of his kingdom at its eastern extremity was the Persian Sea,
at its northern extremity the Pontus Euxinus, at its western extremity the island of Cyprus and Egypt, ⚠3
and at its southern extremity the land of Cush [Ethiopia]. He resided during the seven winter months in the city of Babylon, its climate being good,
the three months of springtime in Susa, and the two summer months in Ecbatana, ⚠4
and in his old age he returned to the cities of Persia, the burial place of his forefathers.

In the seventh year of his reign, one night while he was still asleep, there appeared to him a figure more excellent than a man, who called him by name
and said to him: "Prepare yourself, for you will soon depart to the gods."
When he awoke from his sleep he summoned his sons and his friends and spoke to their hearts good and upright words concerning the vicissitudes of time, the merit of deeds, and the survival of the soul,
and "how good and how pleasant" it is for brothers to be agreeable to one another and "to dwell together" [cf. Ps 133:1].
Of the two sons he had, Cambyses the elder and the second, Tanaoxares, he commanded that the elder should reign in his place, ⚠5
and to the second he gave gifts and revenues befitting his rank; and he expired and died.
After these things, Xenophon wrote that the sons of this Cyrus did not walk in his ways, that quarrels arose between them,
and that all the good qualities the Persian nation had possessed in the days of this Cyrus "proceeded from evil to evil" [Jer 9:2].

In this last chapter he sometimes calls that son of his who sat on his throne by the name Artaxerxes,
this being a royal title, as we wrote at the beginning of chapter 18, ⚠6
for he said: "In the days of Artaxerxes the people were given over to lust and wine."
In the other book, which he composed on Cyrus the Second, he wrote that King Darius had two sons born to him by his wife Parysatis, ⚠7
the elder Artaxerxes and the second Cyrus, and when he sensed that his days drew near to die, he commanded that Artaxerxes should reign.
And since, some time after he had sat on the royal throne,
one of the courtiers slandered Cyrus his brother to him, saying that he sought to lay hands on him,
he put this Cyrus in prison and ordered him put to death, had not his aforementioned mother saved him;
and he, after his release from prison, endeavored to win the people's hearts, killed the king his brother, and sat on his throne in peace and quiet. ⚠8

Thus far the words of Xenophon. The chief reason I have copied these words of his concerns the kings of Persia, about whom there is discussion regarding doubts as to their number and order,
and to show that, according to his account, it is possible that the Artaxerxes written in Ezra 4, "And in the days of Artaxerxes" etc. [Ezra 4:7], who at the request of the adversaries wrote an accusation,
was the base Artaxerxes, son of Cyrus the First, whom he mentioned;
and that this Artaxerxes and the Ahasuerus written there in the preceding verse [Ezra 4:6] came between the reign of Cyrus the First and the reign of Darius mentioned in the verse immediately above it [Ezra 4:5], making four kings.
Further, according to his words, one must also reckon Artaxerxes son of King Darius, and Cyrus the Second who arose after him;
and perhaps this is the "Cyrus king of Persia" written in Daniel at the beginning of chapter 10 [Dan 10:1].
Accordingly, even by the plain sense of the verses, apart from Darius the builder of the Temple and any other king who may perhaps have come after him, the kings of Persia were more than four;
and the saying "Behold, there shall stand yet three kings in Persia" [Dan 11:2] will bear the interpretation you will find for it in chapter 35 below;
nevertheless, it was not founded upon this, as stated.  What may plausibly be derived from the four writers adjoining [these chapters] is to be sought from chapter 35 onward. ⚠9
