In the first chapter of Eduyot we learn in the Mishnah, and likewise it is taught in the Tosefta of Eduyot: "Why do they record the opinion of an individual among [those of] the majority? To set it aside;
so that if a person says to you, 'Thus have I received [it],' one may say to him, 'You heard it as the opinion of so-and-so'" [m. Eduyot 1:6; t. Eduyot 1:4].
Now since I have seen many accounts in Josephus "for the Romans" [the Greek Josephus] that differ from what our rabbis wrote, ⚠1
I thought it right to record some of them, whether in this chapter or in the chapters to come;
and whatever new [insight] I may have on the matter I would value your knowing.
So my subject now is the altar that was built in Alexandria of Egypt, and the high place that was set up on Mount Gerizim,
and the figure of the dove that was found there, and how many King Yannais there were in the time of the [Second] Temple.
From these remarks of mine, and others like them which God in His kindness will yet be with me to add, it will become clear to you
that you see Rabad ha-Levi [Abraham ibn Daud] in his Sefer ha-Qabbalah, or anyone else among the later authorities of blessed memory, writing things not in accordance with the teaching of our Sages, of unknown origin and destination.

Indeed, as to the first [subject], everyone knows what the Sages of blessed memory related in the Jerusalem Talmud, Yoma, second chapter [sic: chapter] "Se'irei", and at the end of tractate Menahot, ⚠2
that Onias son of Simeon the Righteous built the altar in Alexandria of Egypt.
This was because, as they related there, Simeon at the time of his death said, "My son Onias shall serve in my place,"
and a quarrel arose between him and Shimei his brother, which Rabbi Meir related in one way, while Rabbi Judah said to him, "That is not how the incident happened," etc.; see there;
until he went to Alexandria of Egypt and built an altar there [b. Menahot 109b].
And shortly after they said that, according to Rabbi Meir, the sons of kings built another altar there in the days of Hezekiah.
But if you incline your ear to hear how Josephus in his Roman [i.e., Greek] work told that incident, you will understand how great a difference there is between them.
Although I will not take the space here to copy all his words, it will suffice to indicate their location and the main points on which they differ.

In book 12, chapter 3, he wrote that after Simeon the Righteous, Eleazar his brother served as [high] priest, in whose time our Torah was translated;
and after that Eleazar, Manasseh served as priest, the uncle of the brothers Simeon and Eleazar;
and after this Manasseh, Onias son of Simeon the Righteous served as priest.
At the beginning of chapter 6 he wrote that Onias, when he died, left a young son, also named Onias, who because of his youth was not fit for the high priesthood;
and in his place served Jesus [Jason], brother of the father Onias and son of Simeon the Righteous.
And since a dispute arose between this Jesus and his third brother, who was also named Onias and chose to be called Menelaus—all three of them sons of Simeon the Righteous—
this Menelaus, in order to keep the rank of the high priesthood, vowed to Antiochus, who then ruled over them, to bring the majority of the Jewish populace over to the religion of the Greeks.
And in chapter 15 he wrote that after some time, when the aforementioned Antiochus saw that this Menelaus was causing many quarrels among the Jews under his rule, and great trouble to himself on that account,
he killed him and appointed as high priest one of another family, named Jakim [Alcimus], a worthless and wicked man,
as his deeds also appear in both Books of the Maccabees.

And when Onias son of the aforementioned Onias saw that his uncle had been killed, that the rank of the high priesthood had departed from his family, and that the rest of the Jews too were in great distress,
he went off to Ptolemy the Fourth, called Philometor, husband and brother of Cleopatra, who then reigned jointly with him over Egypt. ⚠3
And in chapter 17 he wrote that this Jakim the high priest died a sudden death from Heaven when he sought to demolish a wall built around the Sanctuary by order of the prophets of old,
and that the people then gave the high priesthood to Judah the Hasmonean, called Maccabaeus.
And in book 13, chapter 6, he wrote that Onias son of Onias son of Simeon the Righteous, who as we related went to Egypt and contrived to find favor with the king and queen,
submitted a petition to them that they allow him to erect an altar to the Lord in the province of Egypt,
all the more so since the prophet Isaiah had prophesied about it, saying, "There shall be an altar to the Lord in the midst of the land of Egypt," etc. [Isa 19:19].
They answered him that they in no way wished to act in this against the religion of our God,
but that if by his own full authority they could do it, they granted his request in full force.
And then he built in Alexandria of Egypt a likeness of the Temple in Jerusalem, ⚠4
as Josephus mentioned this briefly in his first book of the Wars, chapter 1,
and at length he wrote of its causes, its forms, and the length of its duration, book 7, chapter 30.
Thus far the affair of Onias in Alexandria. Now anyone with understanding, along with any student, will readily see how these accounts differ in their very substance from the words of our rabbis.

However, concerning the high place erected by the Samaritans on Mount Gerizim, according to what is brought in tractate Yoma, chapter "Ba lo," and in Megillat Ta'anit, chapter 9,
it appears that in the days of Alexander of Macedon it was already built from of old,
and that when the Samaritans asked him to destroy the house of our God in Jerusalem,
Simeon the Righteous went out with a company of priests to meet Alexander, and obtained [permission] to kill those Samaritans, and they ploughed up Mount Gerizim and sowed it with vetch and salt [b. Yoma 69a].
And in the first chapter of Hullin they further related that in the days of Rabbi Meir they found the Samaritans [had] a figure of a dove on the summit of Mount Gerizim, and he decreed that they be treated entirely as gentiles [b. Hullin 6a].
Since there appears some contradiction between these two accounts of theirs—
for according to the first statement that high place was abolished in the days of Simeon the Righteous and Alexander,
whereas according to the second it was still in force in the days of Rabbi Meir—
Ritba of blessed memory, on chapter "Ba lo," resolved it [by saying] that the Samaritans were "lion-converts,"
and indeed in the days of Simeon the Righteous they were accepted as fit, but in the days of Rabbi Meir they reverted to their ways and were disqualified.

But if you read what Josephus wrote in book 11, chapter 8, you will see that in his view this resembles the affair of Alexandria of Egypt mentioned above;
namely, that on the contrary, the very first erection of the high place on Mount Gerizim was by order of Alexander of Macedon and with his permission, for before him it did not exist at all;
and it was he who placed over it Manasseh, brother of Jaddua the high priest and son-in-law of Sanballat the Samaritan, out of love for this Sanballat, who had found favor with Alexander;
and it was destroyed by Hyrcanus the high priest in the two-hundredth year after its building. And Rabad ha-Levi in his Sefer ha-Qabbalah did not depart from the way of this Josephus, as appears in his chapter on the second generation,
and more explicitly in his account of the kings of Israel in the Second Temple period, where he mentioned Hyrcanus and all his deeds.
And Josephus, in book 13, chapter 6, of the Roman [i.e., Greek] work, related that in the days of the aforementioned Ptolemy IV Philometor the Samaritans came to plead before him against the Jerusalemites,
claiming that by right the sanctity of Mount Gerizim was greater than the sanctity of the Temple;
and the envoys of the two parties stipulated between them that whichever lost the case would forfeit his head to the king;
and so the foolish envoys of the Samaritans lost and were punished. And who knows whether this is the "Day of Mount Gerizim" that the Sages of blessed memory mentioned in Yoma, chapter "Ba lo," and chapter 9 of Megillat Ta'anit, cited above.

On this subject I shall not refrain from drawing the reader's attention to what the author of the Tosafot of blessed memory wrote in the first chapter of Hullin on the statement "They found a figure of a dove belonging to them on the summit of Mount Gerizim"; these are his words:
"There is [a statement] in the Midrash that it was the idol that Jacob buried under the terebinth which is by Shechem" [cf. Gen 35:4].
End of quotation. But in my opinion the wise will also brighten their faces at these words of mine.
For, as appears from the accounts of Berosus the Chaldean in his fourth book—and several passages of Scripture also support him, which we shall yet mention in chapter 38 of the third Ma'amar—
the kingdoms of Assyria and Babylon were one from the days of Nimrod until their thirty-eighth king, called Sardanapalus;
and although, according to the accounts of Metasthenes the Persian, which you will yet hear from us, they were afterward divided for a time into two kingdoms,
yet when Darius and Cyrus rose against Babylon they became united as before, and likewise they remained a single union under Alexander of Macedon, king over all the earth, as is known.
And it is well known that among those who held the kingship in ancient times was Semiramis, the fourth from Nimrod,
of whom it is related by several chroniclers, and especially by Diodorus Siculus, book 3, ⚠5
that the birds of the sky brought her food when she was born, and in particular young doves;
and for that reason she is called Semiramis, which (according to him) in the Aramaic tongue signifies the chirping of birds.

In my opinion it derives from the expression "the time of singing [zamir] has come" [Song 2:12], with the zayin replaced by samekh;
and likewise in Aramaic, in the first chapter of Gittin, "singing of instruments, singing of the mouth" [b. Gittin 7a].
Therefore the emblem of her standard was a dove. And since this queen, according to Berosus in the aforementioned book, rose to renown and praise above all the kings of the earth,
the emblem of the standard of Babylon even after her death was always a dove,
as you will find all this at length in the Christian scholar Annius in his commentary on the aforementioned book of Berosus, and also in his commentary on Xenophon's book of Equivocal Names,
and likewise in the Christian Samotheo in his fourth book, chapter 1. They also said, in the name of the Christian scholars Jerome the translator and Thomas Aquinas, that this is [the meaning of] Jeremiah 25: "He has forsaken his covert like a young lion … because of the fierceness of the dove [ha-yonah]" [Jer 25:38], ⚠6
meaning that every king and his people fled and abandoned his dwelling because of the fierceness of the Babylonians.
And likewise in the prophecy on Egypt, Jeremiah 46: "because of the sword of the dove" [Jer 46:16],
he meant that the foreigners who were in Egypt, or the soldiers who came from other lands to help her, would say to one another, "Arise, let us return to our people, because of the sword of Babylon."

And in Jeremiah 50, in the prophecy on Babylon, where he also said, "Cut off the sower from Babylon, and him that handles the sickle in the time of harvest; because of the sword of the dove they shall turn every one to his people, and flee every one to his own land" [Jer 50:16],
the meaning is that the foreign sowers and reapers who came there to help the owners of the fields—as is the custom in many Italian lands rich in agriculture and produce—
would then flee from the sword coming upon her; for in the usage of the language a thing may be referred both to that from which it comes and to that to which it goes, as in "I will make them joyful in My house of prayer" [Isa 56:7] [and] "I have heard your prayer" [2 Kgs 20:5]. ⚠7
Accordingly we may also say that in Zephaniah 3, where he said of Jerusalem, "Woe to her that is rebellious and polluted, the oppressing city [ha-ir ha-yonah]" [Zeph 3:1],
he meant that in her deeds she resembles Babylon, whose standard bore the emblem of a dove, as we mentioned.
And there were some nations that so loved the memory of Semiramis and her emblem
that they forbade themselves to eat young doves, as Tibullus, the gentile poet, wrote in his first book, Elegy 7.
Also Yedidyah the Alexandrian [Philo], according to what Eusebius cited at the end of book 8 of his Praeparatio,
wrote in a fine work of which we have only the little that he [Eusebius] set before us there,
that when he traveled from Alexandria of Egypt to Jerusalem he passed through the land of Ashkelon (so indeed you will find in Maimonides, chapter 5 of the Laws of Sanctification of the New Moon: between Egypt and Jerusalem by way of Ashkelon is a journey of eight days),
and he saw young doves accompanying and mingling with people without fear or dread;
and this was because their custom from of old was not to kill any of them.

And according to their invented falsehoods, as the aforementioned Annius mentioned, Semiramis was the daughter of the idol Dagon, whose form was a fish,
and accordingly her lineage name was Semiramis Ascalonita. Because of all these things it seems likely to me that the Samaritans, whom the king of Assyria and Babylon settled in Samaria,
as a sign and testimony of their loving submission to that king of theirs, the emblem of whose standard was a dove,
cherished it and set it up as an object of worship on the summit of Mount Gerizim, which is by Shechem, the capital of the kingdom of Samaria in those days, as Josephus wrote in his Roman [i.e., Greek] work at the end of book 11.
As for Yannai, you will find that the Sages of blessed memory mention three King Yannais during the period of the [Second] Temple:
one, in chapter "Yesh noḥalin," in the days of Yose ben Yo'ezer,
for they said: "The son of Yose ben Yo'ezer married the daughter of the one who plaited crowns for King Yannai," etc. [b. Bava Batra 133b],
and Rashi explained: the daughter of one who weaves crowns for King Yannai. Another, in Berakhot, chapter "Tefillat ha-shaḥar" and chapter "Sheloshah she-akhlu,"
and Sotah, last chapter, and chapter "Ḥelek," and Miqqets Rabbah, and Midrash Qohelet on the verse "Wisdom is good" [Eccl 7:11],
who lived in the days of Simeon ben Shetah, his brother-in-law; and it is already known from the first chapter of Avot that this Simeon was the third generation after Yose ben Yo'ezer,
and had he lived in the days of the said Yose, he would not have received his Torah from Joshua and Nittai, his [Yose's] disciples, but from him.
And this Yannai mentioned in Berakhot, as stated, is in my opinion the one also mentioned there in chapter "Keitsad mevarekhin," and in Kiddushin, chapter "Ha-omer," and Megillat Ta'anit, chapter 11.

And another, several years later, close to the second destruction, as appears in Yoma, first chapter, and Yevamot, chapter 6,
that Martha daughter of Boethus (who according to what is found in chapter "Ha-nizakin" and Eikhah Rabbah on the verse "For these things" [Lam 1:16] lived at the time of the destruction) brought King Yannai a tarqav-measure of dinars until they appointed Joshua ben Gamla among the high priests [b. Yoma 18a; b. Yevamot 61a].
And Ritba wrote there: "This is not the Yannai of chapter 'Ha-omer,' for he was high priest and did not give his priesthood to others; rather, there were two Yannais." End of quotation.
Thus, together with the Yannai of "Yesh noḥalin" mentioned above, about whom Ritba had no need to speak, there are three.
Now in all of Josephus's accounts in the Roman [i.e., Greek] work you will find only that Yannai whom the Sages mentioned in chapter "Ha-omer," regarding Eleazar ben Po'irah, who said to King Yannai the high priest, "The hearts of the Pharisees are against you" [b. Kiddushin 66a],
because a false rumor went out about his mother that she had been taken captive at Mount Modi'in and that he was unfit for the high priesthood.
This is the incident that Josephus recorded in chapter 18 of book 13, but with this difference: the Sages attributed that event to Yannai,
while Josephus attributed it to Hyrcanus his father. And in chapter "Tefillat ha-shaḥar" you will see that they were in doubt about the name Yannai:
Abaye said, "Yannai is the same as Yohanan," and Rava said, "Yannai is one and Yohanan another" [b. Berakhot 29a].

And Yedidyah in the Book of Times, which you will yet see in chapter 32 of these chapters of mine, and Josephus, chapter 2 of book 13, mentioned Yannai Alexander,
and wrote that John, first of the Hasmoneans [rulers], was also called Hyrcanus,
and that he was the father of Yannai Alexander, husband of Queen Alexandra,
whom at the time of his death he advised to turn from the Sadducees to the Pharisees, for they would strengthen her hands in her reign,
as Josephus brought in book 13, chapter 23, and likewise Rabad in his Qabbalah.
It appears that the Sages of blessed memory alluded to these accounts of the said husband and wife in chapter "Hayah notel" when they said: "King Yannai said to his wife, 'Fear neither the Pharisees nor those who are not Pharisees, but the hypocrites,'" etc. [b. Sotah 22b].
And likewise, in my opinion, these are the Yannai and his wife mentioned in Megillat Ta'anit, chapter 11, [where it is said] that before his death he commanded the prison warden to put the elders of Israel to death, so that all the people of the land would mourn at his death and not rejoice.
And Salminon his wife mentioned there is Salome Alexandra, for Josephus wrote in chapter 20 of book 13 that Salome in Greek is Alexandra. ⚠8

However, Josephus in book 17, chapter 8, attributed this furious testament to Hyrcanus, who close to his death charged Salome his sister with it, she too being called Alexandra. ⚠9
It is true that in the aforementioned Book of Times you will find among the princes of the house of David a Yannai called Hyrcanus II, to distinguish him from Judah I, also called Hyrcanus;
and in my opinion this is the Yannai of "Yesh noḥalin," who was not a priest but a prince (or, say, a ruler), the fourth from Zerubbabel,
and his time corresponds to the time of Yose ben Yo'ezer. Moreover, this same Yedidyah, in his account of the high priests, mentioned Yannai Alexander, as stated,
so that there were two Yannais: namely, the king of the house of David who lived in the days of Yose ben Yo'ezer,
and the high priest of the Hasmonean house who lived in the days of Simeon ben Shetah.
But as for that Yannai of tractate Yoma, close to the destruction, there is no one among all the writers of the [annals] of the house of Herod, who reigned then, who mentions him; ⚠10
nor will you find among them any Hyrcanus, John, or Alexander.

And one cannot say that this Yannai of Yoma is none other than the one of Simeon ben Shetah, and that Martha daughter of Boethus is a different one,
since Joshua ben Gamla her husband lived at the time of the destruction;
and Josephus, book 20, chapter 8, wrote that King Agrippa, who lived in the days of the emperor Nero, raised Joshua ben Gamla to the high priesthood after Jesus son of Damnaeus.
And concerning this Agrippa—of whom they wrote in chapter "Elu ne'emarin" that the Jewish populace flattered him, saying, "Fear not, Agrippa, you are our brother" [b. Sotah 41b; m. Sotah 7:8]—
Rashi of blessed memory wrote: "Agrippa king of Israel was of the seed of Herod, and it was in his days that the Temple was destroyed."
For these reasons I say that the said Martha brought the tarqav-measure not to Yannai but to Agrippa,
and the mention of Yannai here is an error which is doubtless to be attributed to some early copyist,
as one should likewise judge regarding the many confusions found in some of the Sages' statements on chronological accounts;
and reliable testimony to the truth is that the mind is at ease with it.
