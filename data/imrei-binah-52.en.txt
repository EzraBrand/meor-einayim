In those days Helena, queen of the Adiabenians, and Izates her son entered the religion of the Jews, as follows: ⚠1
Monobazus, king of the Adiabenians, surnamed Bazaeus, became enamored of his sister; she became his wife and conceived.
Now it happened once, as he lay beside her, that his hand was spread over her belly,
and it seemed to him that he heard a voice speaking to him and commanding him to lift his hand from her belly, lest it weigh on the child,
[saying] that just as its beginning had not been without God's will, so its end would be exceedingly prosperous.
Monobazus was astonished at this voice, and as soon as he woke he told the matter to his wife.
When she bore [the child], he called his name Izates. Now this Monobazus of whom we spoke had earlier had by this same wife a son named Monobazus after himself,
and other sons were also born to him by other wives besides these.
Yet all who knew him easily recognized that his whole affection was given to Izates alone,
and therefore his brothers began to envy him, and from day to day their hatred of him grew, each of them fearing that Izates surpassed him in their father's love.

When all this became known to King Monobazus, he judged those sons of his charitably,
for he said: surely this feeling of theirs comes not from malice of heart but from their great desire to be loved by him as he [Izates] was.
Nevertheless, fearing for Izates on account of his brothers' hatred, he sent him with many gifts to Abennerigos, then ruler of a city called by its inhabitants Spasini, and asked him to watch over the boy.
He received him with a cheerful countenance, and since he grew attached to him in love, he gave him in marriage a daughter named Samacho,
and as a dowry gave them together a fertile country from which they could draw bread to the full.
One day King Monobazus felt old age leaping upon him and his soul longed to see his son before he went the way of all the earth.
He sent and summoned him, received him with exceeding affection, and gave him a tract of land called Carron, which grows abundant amomum trees,
and on it are preserved the remains of the ark in which, as is told, Noah was saved from the waters of the Flood; it is still visible there today to every eye that looks.
And Izates dwelt in that land until the death of the king his father.

On the day he died, Helena his widow summoned the governors and the army commanders and said to them:
You surely know the mind of the king my husband, that Izates should be heir to the throne, since he judged that he was worthy of this honor;
yet I long to know also what seems good to you, since I have reckoned that he who sits on the throne is confirmed not by the judgment of one but by the many who choose him.
This question of hers was put to them in order to discern to which of them all the assembly's agreement would turn.
When the nobles heard her words, before answering them they bowed down to her as one reigning over the land;
afterwards they said that they indeed ratified whatever she might decree,
and that they would gladly accept Izates as king over them, since they knew that with his father, and by the consent of all the people, he had been chosen above all his brothers for the excellence of his conduct.
They also offered her, if it pleased her, first to kill his brothers and their kinsmen, so as to secure for the new king a royal throne firmly established,
for in this way he would be free of any fear he might have of their hatred and envy on this account.

In response to these words Helena thanked them all and showed them favor, saying that they had indeed given proof of loyal love;
but she would decide nothing about killing the brothers, for she said: let my son come, and he will pass his own sentence on them.
Then the nobles advised her that in any case it would be good meanwhile to gather them into custody, so that the kingdom might be more secure for her son,
and that until his coming she should appoint whomever she saw fit to oversee the administration of the land.
Their words pleased Helena, and she chose Monobazus her elder son to be the appointed regent they had agreed upon;
accordingly the royal diadem was placed on his head, and she gave him the ring of the king his father and his scepter, and charged him to manage affairs until his brother Izates came.
Izates, on hearing of his father's death, did not delay in coming, and with the consent of Monobazus his brother he became king over them all.
Now at the time when Izates was living near the city of Spasini,
a Jewish merchant named Ananias had access among the women of that ruler and taught them to worship God according to the law of the Jews;
and through these women he won the friendship of Izates, whose heart too was moved toward this belief.
When he was called to return to the land of the Adiabenians, [Ananias] also accompanied him, for he pressed him until he agreed to go.
Likewise it happened that Helena too, having been taught by another Jew, cleaved to the law of God and its commandments.

When Izates came to the land of his people the Adiabenians and found his brothers and their kinsmen in prison, he was greatly displeased,
for to kill them or to leave them perpetually in custody did not seem right to him, yet if they went free there was danger that they would turn hostile and take revenge on him;
so he sent some of them with their own sons to the emperor Claudius at Rome, and some to Artabanus king of the Parthians, to remain in either place as hostages of the Adiabenians.
Afterwards, perceiving that his mother took great delight in the religion of the Jews, he chose to pass over to their law as she had.
And since he judged that he was not truly a Jew until he circumcised the flesh of his foreskin, his heart was inclined to remove its impurity from himself.
But when the matter became known to his mother she strove hard to turn him from this intention, telling him that his danger would only increase, that his people would gather against him and murder him,
since his worshipping other than their gods would be a great cause to turn their hearts from him, and they would by no means endure a Jew as their king.
Therefore his mother restrained him for some days and prevailed on him not to carry out his wish in this matter.

His friend Ananias too, whom he consulted, did not depart from Helena's line,
and threatened him that if he did not desist from this intention he would depart and leave him,
for he feared that when these hidden things were revealed to the multitude all the blame would come down upon his head,
for they would say: you are the one who brought this about by teaching the king the law and practice of your people.
He also told him that in truth he might worship God while uncircumcised,
so long as he cleaved to the law of the Jews with a whole heart and all his strength,
since the worship he sought depended more on the perfecting of the soul than on the fitness of the body, which is of lower rank,
and that God would pardon him, since he was compelled to forgo this covenant lest the multitude rise in strife and the whole land be turned to tumult and turmoil;
so that for many days, by these words, he dissuaded him from putting his wish into action.

But afterwards, while the spirit of desire still stirred in him,
another Jew, a Galilean named Eleazar, whose name was great among those versed in the Torah,
drew his heart to complete the commandment he had begun.
For once, having been brought to pay his respects to him and finding him reading in the book of the Torah of Moses,
he said to him: My lord the king, do you not perceive how greatly you wrong this Torah, and through it God?
For it is not enough to know His ways; one must keep His commandments.
How long will you bear the reproach of your foreskin? If you have not yet read the commandment of circumcision, rise and read it, that you may know how much harm there is in neglecting it.
When the king heard his words he did not delay to fulfill his duty; he withdrew into an inner chamber, summoned a skilled physician, and had the flesh of his foreskin circumcised at once.
Afterwards he called his mother and his teacher Ananias and told them: such and such have I done.

Then fear and trembling seized them, lest when the matter became known to the people they would be the first to thrust Izates from the throne, for the nations would not endure this outrage, that a man who worshipped a god foreign to the land should reign over them;
and they also feared for themselves, that with one voice [the people] would bring charges against them, saying: you consented to this deed of his and even helped him in it.
But it was brought about by God that these fears of theirs did not befall them, for He delivered Izates and his sons, with all he had, from many dangers,
and from many troubles which they thought beyond remedy He brought them out into abundant mercy,
until it was made known to them that all who take refuge in Him and give themselves to Him alone shall eat the fruit of their trust.
But let us leave these matters now, for they will shortly be set out by us.
Now Helena the king's mother, seeing the affairs of the kingdom peaceful and quiet, and her son, with God's help, beloved and blessed by all who knew him, stranger and native alike,
longed to see Jerusalem and the Temple, whose fame had grown great in all lands, to worship God there and to pay before Him the burnt offerings of her vows.

Since she easily obtained permission for this from the king her son, after ample preparations for the journey, having also made herself great provisions, she went to Jerusalem, her son accompanying her for some days to show her the way.
Her arrival then happened most opportunely for the people of the land.
For since at that time the famine was severe in the city and many were dying for lack of food,
Queen Helena hastened to send some of her servants to Alexandria to bring grain and bread from there, and some to Cyprus to fetch dried figs, and all of them hurried back,
and she distributed what they brought among the poor of the land's inhabitants,
and through this kindness of hers she earned among our people an everlasting memorial that will not be forgotten.
Likewise Izates her son, seeing that people in the distress of the great famine,
hastened to send a large sum of gold denarii to their leaders in Jerusalem.

As for the many kindnesses with which these two sovereigns did abundant good to our people,
we shall speak of them further below. Now it is to be told that in those days Artabanus king of the Parthians perceived that his nobles, his ministers, were ready to rebel against him,
and since he no longer dwelt securely in his land, he chose to entrust himself to Izates and seek deliverance from him, in the hope that he might restore him to his kingdom in peace and quiet.
So he took with him some thousand of his kinsmen and trusted household and went and met him on the road,
for he indeed knew [Izates], but [Izates] did not know him, never having seen him.
When he came near him, before opening his mouth, he bowed down before him according to the custom of the country,
and then began to pour out his plea in these words: Alas, my king, let not your servant who entreats your majesty be despised in your eyes,
and do not abhor the affliction of the poor man who has no recompense to offer for being helped by you.

For indeed, cast down from the height of kingship to lowliness of station, I have fled to the shelter of your help.
Therefore consider how suddenly the wheel turns upon the order of human affairs,
and reflect also in your own case, from your own knowledge, to how many reversals we are all subject.
If now you abandon me without avenging me of my foes,
will not the servants who break away from their kings multiply? And who will dare to dwell securely?
So the man spoke to him, tears on his cheeks and his face to the ground.
Then King Izates, having heard him name himself and seeing that Artabanus, a great king, stood begging for his life,
came down from his chariot and said to him: O my lord the king, be strong and of good courage; let not your heart fail you because of this trouble, as though it had no healing remedy.
Your grief will suddenly pass, and you will find a friend and companion better for you than perhaps you have hoped until now.
I will not rest until I restore you to the kingdom of the Parthians, or else I myself shall go out of my own kingdom, removing crown and diadem.

After these words he set Artabanus on his chariot while he himself kept him company on foot, walking along the road,
for he thought that a king greater than himself was worthy of such honor.
But this was most loathsome to Artabanus,
and he swore by the deliverance he longed for in that distress, and by the dignity of the crown, that he would at once get down and walk on foot unless [Izates] too mounted his chariot and went before him on the way.
Then, out of regard for him, [Izates] mounted the chariot, and at a gentle pace brought him to his royal palace and chambers, honored him with royal delicacies, and seated him at the head of his guests,
for he looked not at his present state but at his former rank,
and considered how what had now befallen him might suddenly befall anyone among the proud.
Besides this, he undertook to write to the Parthian nobles and spoke persuasively to them not to be slow in restoring their king,
[assuring them] that he would indeed forget all they had done to him; and by a binding oath he removed from their hearts all fear and trembling.

Those nobles were not unwilling to restore him, but they excused themselves that they could not, since they had already given the kingship to another, named Kinnamos,
and they feared that if the first ruler returned to his place, war would break out among the inhabitants of the land.
But this Kinnamos, perceiving the will of the princes—he had been raised on Artabanus's knees and was also by nature an honest and upright man—
wrote to him to return on his assurance that his crown would flourish upon him as in days of old; and he trusted him and returned.
Kinnamos went out to meet him and bowed down to the ground, and, being addressed by him as king, removed the crown from his head and placed it on the head of Artabanus.
Thus with Izates' help he returned in peace to the kingdom from which he had been driven out.
Nor did he forget the kindnesses he had received from him, for he greatly honored Izates according to the customs of those lands:
he permitted him to wear, as he himself did, an upright tiara and to sleep on a golden bed,
privileges reserved as marks of honor to the Parthian kings. He also gave him a good and broad land called Nisibis, which he had earlier taken from the king of Armenia,
in which, in former days, the Macedonians had founded the city of Antioch called Mygdonia.
All these Izates received from the king of the Parthians for all the good he had done him.

Some years later Artabanus ended his days and left his kingdom to his son Vardanes.
This Vardanes planned to make war on the Romans and went to Izates to ask him to make a league with him,
but he could not accomplish his purpose. For Izates, who knew the might and strength of the Roman empire, regarded it all as striving after wind,
besides the fact that he had already sent five of his sons to learn our language and the laws of our people, and he was then also bereft of his mother, who had gone to worship and sacrifice in Jerusalem.
Therefore his heart was not inclined at that time to go out to battle,
but he endeavored to dissuade Vardanes from this plan by recounting to him the deeds of the Romans and the might of their hand, so as to make him afraid to engage.
But Vardanes, who had fixed his resolve on this plan, found all Izates' words to be a pricking brier,
and suddenly smoke rose from his nostrils with wrathful thoughts of pouring his fury upon Izates, and he spoke harshly to him, saying,
"Increase your army and come out" [cf. Judg 9:29]; but his intent was vain and false, for quickly, by God's will, all his foundations were destroyed.

For when the Parthian lords sensed Vardanes' intention to fight the Roman armies, they rose against him, killed him, and transferred his kingdom to his brother Gotarzes;
he too within a few days was killed by treachery, and Vologeses reigned in his stead,
who divided his kingdom between two brothers, twins of the womb: to Pacorus the elder he gave Media, and to Tiridates the younger he gave Armenia.
In those days it happened that Monobazus the king's brother and his other kinsmen, seeing that Izates, because he was devoted to the worship of God, prospered in all he did and that his name was renowned throughout the earth,
resolved that it would be good for them all too to abandon the idol worship in which they had been raised and to keep the law of the Jews.
When a whisper of this reached the ears of the lords, they were very angry,
but they restrained themselves from revealing their hearts' secret, waiting for a pretext to be found to accuse him of rebellion and treachery.
So they wrote to Abias king of the Arabs, with a large sum of silver which they pledged to him if he would come and make war on Izates,
and they swore to him that as soon as his army approached them they would turn their backs on their king,
since all the inclination of their hearts was to take revenge on him for abandoning the covenant of the land's forefathers.
And after binding themselves to him by compact, they urged him with all vigor to hasten his action.

Abias agreed and went out with a large force to make war on Izates.
When his army appeared at the city, the people did not wait for him to come near to fight,
but at once betrayed their king, and as if sudden trembling had seized them, they were all terrified before the enemy and hastened to flee.
Yet for all that Izates' heart did not fail him; but perceiving that the nobles had a hand in the treachery,
he went into battle in person, and inquired and investigated thoroughly who were the authors of the treacherous words and the heads of the conspiracy;
and he seized many of those who had agreed with the enemy to go over to his protection, and executed judgment on them, hanging them all together before the sun.
On the morrow he girded his loins like a man and fought his enemies, and after many of them had fallen slain before him, a terror from God fell upon the rest and they fled.
Pursuing their king himself, he blocked the roads against him, so that he had to flee to the fortress of Arsamus;
and he pressed his assault against the wall until he captured that place, and with much wealth and spoil that he took from it he returned to his land Adiabene with songs and timbrel and glory.
But he could not take Abias alive, for, lest he become a captive in the hand of a foe, he chose for himself the bitterness of death.

The Adiabenian lords, however, although this time God had frustrated their counsel and delivered their leaders into Izates' hand,
did not refrain from devising new evil against him and preparing yet another net for his steps.
For they wrote to Vologeses king of the Parthians that he should seek with all his strength to destroy him from before them, for then he would set over them a king of Parthian birth, "the son of Tabeel" [cf. Isa 7:6],
telling him that they could by no means bear as king Izates, who had exchanged their glory for another god.
On hearing these words he gladly agreed and resolved to make war on him,
and seeking a pretext against him he demanded that he return to him those lands which Artabanus his father had formerly given him as a perpetual gift for the kindnesses Izates had done him,
and [warned] that if he would not obey, he should know that a day of darkness was prepared for him by his hand.
At the sound of this message Izates was greatly shaken, and it seemed to him a cause for contempt and wrath to return out of fear what he had acquired through honor;
and although he knew that even if he did the enemy's will he would still not relent and return his sword to its sheath,
he resolved to cast all his burden upon God,
for this time all he possessed was placed for him in the balance.

His heart confident in this support, he placed his sons and wives in one of the fortified cities,
gathered all the produce into the towers, then burned all the provisions and crops of the soil that remained outside them around the cities, and then waited for [the enemy] to encamp against him.
When the king of the Parthians came with his large force and strong hand, more swiftly than anyone would have imagined,
he pitched his royal tents on the river that separates the land of Media from Adiabene.
Izates too stationed his forces at a place not far from him,
having with him six thousand horsemen, all bearing swords. When the camps had settled in the place we mentioned, the king of the Parthians sent ahead a messenger to tell Izates of the size of his army and the might of his hand,
for he had gathered every sword-wielding young man throughout the breadth of the lands of his kingdom, from the city of Ecbatana to the river Euphrates,
and to threaten him that he would bear his sin for his ingratitude toward his lord,
and that even the God on whom he relied would not be able to save him this time.

The messenger omitted nothing of all his king had commanded him, and spoke to him all those words.
Then Izates answered and said that indeed it was not hidden from him that the army of the Parthian king was too great and mighty for him to clash with,
but that he also knew that God is great and mighty in power above all men on the face of the whole earth.
So he sent the messenger back on his way. Then, having proclaimed fasting and weeping for himself, his sons, and the members of his household, he threw himself to the ground with hearth-ashes on his head and entreated God in these words:
O God, my God, if not in vain I have been diligent to commit myself to the hand of Your goodness, and have called You alone, Most High over all (as indeed is fitting), by the name of Lord of all the earth,
hasten, I pray, deliverance for me—not to protect me from my enemies, but for the honor of Your great name,
that You may humble the arrogance of their uncircumcised heart, who with base tongue have dared to hurl words of destruction against the might of Your hand.
God then saw his affliction and heard his supplication, and that night letters came to Vologeses saying that the hand of the Dahae and the Sacae had spread widely through all the Parthian territory, there being no king to stand in the breach;
so all of a sudden he abandoned the tents as they were, without carrying out his purpose, and returned with all his armies to the cities of the kingdom.
And to all the peoples of the land it was plainly seen that this sudden deliverance came to him through God's providence over Izates.

Not many days afterwards Izates' end drew near, and at fifty-five years of age, in the twenty-fourth year of his reign, his spirit and soul were gathered to God.
And although five sons survived him, he made his elder brother Monobazus king in his stead, to repay him for his kindness in preserving the kingdom for him
when formerly Izates himself was in a foreign land at the death of Monobazus, the father of them all, as we have written.
When Helena his mother heard in Jerusalem of the death of her son, the delight of her eyes, her soul melted with grief,
but she found fitting consolation for herself in knowing that Monobazus her elder son had risen to reign in his stead.
Within a few days she came to see him, and not long after her return to the land of the Adiabenians she too was gathered to her people, a few years after the death of Izates.
Monobazus her son sent her bones and the bones of Izates his brother to Jerusalem, to be buried in the tomb of the three towers, which she had hewn for herself while she was there, about three bowshots from the city.
But the rest of the deeds of this Monobazus and what befell him in the days of his reign will be written in a place more suited to its subject.

— And in those days, when Cuspius Fadus, the Roman commander, was governor over the land of the Jews,
a certain diviner named Theudas incited the multitude to take whatever they could of their best possessions and follow him to the bank of the Jordan,
for he boasted himself a prophet and said that the river would split before him and they would easily cross on foot.
Therefore many were drawn after these lies of his. But Fadus, ruler of the land, did not leave this folly of theirs unpunished,
for he immediately sent against them a troop of horsemen, each with his weapon in hand, who killed many of them and took many alive and brought them to Fadus;
Theudas the sorcerer they also killed by the sword along with their slain, and brought his head to Jerusalem.
These were the events of the Jews in the days of this commander Cuspius Fadus.

— Since I saw in Bereshit Rabbah, parashah 46, on the commandment of circumcision, a story which you will surely say is the same as that told by Josephus, I have thought it worthwhile to copy it alongside it, for thereby the account becomes more credible.
For if differences are found between them, every man of understanding will ascribe the error to the transmitter of one of the two writers mentioned,
for it is he who caused the damage, carrying the sheaves of his reports, straw together with grain, like sheaves to the threshing floor.
At any rate, the two stories will be one for us. They said there, in these words:

It once happened that King Monobazus and Zotos (who is no doubt Izates), sons of King Ptolemy, were sitting and reading the book of Genesis.
When they reached the verse "and you shall circumcise the flesh of your foreskin" [Gen 17:11], this one turned his face to the wall and began to weep, and that one turned his face to the wall and wept.
Both went and were circumcised. Some days later they were sitting and reading the book of Genesis; when they reached this verse, "and you shall circumcise," etc., one said to the other: Woe to you, my brother!
He said to him: Woe to you, not woe to me! They revealed the matter to each other.
When their mother perceived it, she went and said to their father: Your sons have developed a sore [numi] (the Arukh explains: a wart) on their flesh, and the physician has ordered that they be circumcised.
He said to her: Let them be circumcised. How did the Holy One, blessed be He, repay him? Said R. Pinhas: When he went out to war, they set against him a band of pistion (the Arukh explains: ambushes), and an angel came down and saved him. Thus far.
Now let the wise man see and judge that in both versions of the story all goes to one place,
and, as our Sages of blessed memory said in chapter Ha-Mekanne, "words of truth are recognizable" [b. Sotah 9b].

If so, then Rashi and the Tosafists of blessed memory, who wrote that Monobazus and Helena his mother were of the house of the Hasmonean priests, as we mentioned in the previous chapter,
may each of them excuse himself by saying that he heard such a thing but did not see these [sources].
In my opinion, the mention of Ptolemy in this matter is puzzling;
perhaps it is like that Ptolemy in Bereshit Rabbah at the end of parashah 10, whom I mentioned in the sixth chapter of this book.
— Know that Josephus speaks further of the splendid tombs of Helena and her sons mentioned, in the sixth [book] of the Wars, chapter 6, in describing the situation of Jerusalem and its environs. ⚠3
Likewise in book 7, chapter 13, he speaks of a fine palace that belonged to Helena's heirs in Jerusalem,
[recounting] that when the city was delivered into the hand of Titus and his soldiers, who mercilessly set fire to many of its palaces,
the sons of King Izates and his brothers, together with [other] nobles, begged Titus to save them;
and although he was then in great wrath at many who had rebelled against him,
he regarded them graciously, recognizing them as of noble lineage, and made that house of theirs safe from fear.
Surely this is the heritage of the servants of the Lord, blessed forever, selah.

— As for the subject of the previous chapter: now that you, reader, have seen from the beginning of this one that Monobazus, the father of them all, was called Bazaeus,
you may learn that Monobazus too was a royal title, like Pharaoh for the kings of Egypt and Abimelech for the rulers of Philistia, ⚠4
so that the one who "squandered [bizbez] the treasuries," whom our Sages mentioned, may have been Izates, who was likewise called Monobazus by virtue of being king.
And this fits what is told of him regarding the donations he sent to the people of Jerusalem to keep them alive in the famine after the envoys of Helena his mother, as written above;
or it may have been Monobazus his brother, who reigned after him, as we mentioned.
Now the descendants of Monobazus who settled in Jerusalem after their conversion, as stated,
are those remembered for good by our Sages in Niddah, chapter Kol ha-Yad, where they said: Those of the house of King Monobazus did three things, and they are mentioned for praise:
they examined their beds by day (according to the interpretation [that this refers to times] when they do not have relations), they examined them with milla parhava (the Arukh explains: clean wool), and they observed impurity and purity in the snow [b. Niddah 17a].

Likewise regarding the mezuzah, in tractate Menahot, although they concluded that if one hung it on a staff there is no fulfillment of the commandment,
they said that those of the house of King Monobazus did so in their inns, as a remembrance of the mezuzah [b. Menahot 32b].

and this is one of the reasons for the saying of the Sages of blessed memory in chapter Yuhasin [lit. 'Asarah Yuhasin']: "Converts are as hard for Israel as a scab" [b. Kiddushin 70b]. ⚠5
Also, although these are words of conjecture, it is possible that one of the descendants of these converts, who was called Monobazus after his ancestors, ⚠6
