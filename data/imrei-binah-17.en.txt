Among the [rabbinic] sayings, I would very much like you to consider this one. It does not come from ancient tradition, nor did the Sages, of blessed memory, believe it to be compelled by Scripture. It follows rather the useful principle they disclosed to us in the expression "I am expounding Scripture" [lit. "I read a verse and expound it"], as we also wrote in the name of the Riaz in the preceding chapter.
It is the one now set before you; remember it, for a purpose you will yet come to know.
In the chapter Ben Sorer [u-Moreh] they said: "The early generations used to beget at the age of eight years." [b. Sanhedrin 69b] ⚠1
"From where do we know this? Shall we say from what is written, 'Is this not Bathsheba' etc.? How [does that follow]? Perhaps etc.
Rather, from here: 'These are the generations of Terah' etc. How [does that follow]? Perhaps etc. Rather, from here: 'And Bezalel the son of Uri' etc."
First of all, you can see that the author of the saying goes searching for verses from which to prove his words,
and only with difficulty found something to hang them on, in the account of Bezalel, the grandson of Caleb. In chapter 14 I informed you of what all the plain-sense commentators wrote about it. ⚠2
Next, hear with your own ears what they said. To all appearances their intention is to teach the praise of the early generations: that their strength was greater than ours and their nature stronger than ours.
Yet this works against itself, "a deceitful bow" that turns back [Ps 78:57]. For the narratives of the Torah have already made clear that whoever hastens to die hastens to beget, and the reverse.

Consider the ten generations from Adam to Noah,
and likewise, by gradual decline, the ten generations from Noah to Abraham (since the Flood, according to Ramban at the end of Parashat Bereshit, weakened the air and the things that grow in it),
and you will readily understand that the matter is as we have said.
There is also a glorious verse about the hoped-for time when God will renew His world as in days of old: "for the youth shall die a hundred years old" [Isa 65:20].
This is likewise clear from the words of Rashi, of blessed memory, on the verse "Shall a child be born to one who is a hundred years old?" [Gen 17:17], where he wrote as follows:
"Although the early generations begot at five hundred years, in the days of Abraham the years were diminished, weakness came into the world, and they begot sooner."
End of quotation. We can likewise prove this from the words of Rav in the chapter Ha-Ba al Yevimto, on the Mishnah "If a man married a woman and stayed with her ten years" etc. [b. Yevamot 64b],
where he said: "They taught this only of the early generations, whose years were many (and who were slow to beget);
but in the later generations, whose years are few (and who are quick to beget), [the period is] two and a half years."
And although Rava said there that these rules do not hold, for the reason he gave, namely that the years were already diminished in the days of Rabbi, who wrote down the Mishnah, and Rabbi did not distinguish between the one [generation] and the other,
Rav's statement in itself remains firm and sound, speaking what is right.

In Bekhorot, the chapter Ha-Loke'ah, you will also find a passage of the Tosafot concerning a three-year-old cow that calved,
and likewise in the chapter Ein Ma'amidin, the passage beginning "A cow and an ass three years old" etc. From these it is proven, even for kinds of beasts, that early breeding and weakness of strength come together.
Consider further what I tell you. Their statement that the early generations begot at eight years is something unnatural,
and from their other statements, of blessed memory, you can prove that a male does not beget even at eleven, not even as an anomaly.
For at the end of the Gemara of Yotze Dofen and in the chapter Mi She-Met we find the law of the minor boy and girl and the time at which they reach majority,
as Rambam wrote in chapter 2 of the Laws of Marriage. Likewise from the laws of the affliction [fasting] of children, in Yoma, the chapter Yom ha-Kippurim, we know that the male reaches majority one year later than the female.
The female is reckoned a minor until twelve years and one day,
and the male until thirteen. This is because the male's temperament, as Rambam wrote in his commentary on the Mishnah of Yom ha-Kippurim mentioned above, is hotter and more fluid,
while the female attains her settled constitution sooner. In both cases there is an explicit condition that they have produced two hairs,
which are the signs of the onset of majority. On the other hand, it is clear from the first chapter of Yevamot, from the baraita taught by Rav Bivi, "Three women use an absorbent," as Rashi and the Ran, of blessed memory, explained it [b. Yevamot 12b],
that a girl under eleven years and one day certainly will not conceive, not even as an anomaly,
and that in her eleventh year and one day, should she conceive, she may well die, as we shall also explain below about the natural course of women who give birth before their time.
This agrees with the passage in the chapter Yotze Dofen: "Our Rabbis taught: It once happened that Yustini, daughter of Asverus, came before Rabbi. She said to him: Rabbi, at what age may a woman be married? He said to her: At three years and one day.
And at what age does she conceive? He said to her: At twelve years and one day.
She said to him: I was married at six and gave birth at seven; woe for the three years I wasted in my father's house!" [b. Niddah 45a]
And they objected: "But can she conceive [so young]? Did not Rav Bivi teach: Three women use an absorbent:
a minor, lest she conceive and die; a pregnant woman, lest she turn her fetus into a sandal;
a nursing woman, lest she wean her child and it die. And who is a minor? From eleven years and one day until twelve years and one day.
Younger or older than this, she has intercourse in the normal way. These are the words of R. Meir.
But the Sages say: Both this one and that one have intercourse in the normal way, and Heaven will have mercy."
It answers: "If you wish, say: 'whose flesh is the flesh of asses' [Ezek 23:20] (but a daughter of Israel does not conceive so),
and if you wish, say: that girl's mouth spoke falsehood."
End of quotation. From these two premises it follows that the male, who is later than the female as stated,
does not beget even in his eleventh year and one day, not even as an anomaly.
This is also the statement of the sage Ibn Ezra on Parashat Va-Yeshev: "Onan grew until he had seed,"
and this would be no less than twelve years. From what Mizrahi says in his novellae on Parashat Va-Yakhel, on the saying "Hur was the son of Miriam," you will also understand that in our case we do not infer the male minor from the female minor,
since, according to the Sages' own words in the Ben Sorer passage mentioned above, her strength is sounder than his.

And although we say in the chapter Yotze Dofen that a minor boy of nine years and one day who has intercourse with his yevamah acquires her [b. Niddah 45a],
the reason there is that with a yevamah we take account of every act of intercourse, even a sterile one, since it is written "he shall come in unto her" [Deut 25:5].
With any other woman, however, we hold, in the chapter Ha-Ishah Rabbah, that a minor's intercourse counts for nothing until he matures.
Hence even as an anomaly he will not beget under twelve years and one day,
and if he begets in his thirteenth year and one day, it is considered an anomaly and is rarely found. For in the natural course the power of generation does not begin to stir in him until he is fourteen and has already produced the signs of majority,
and even then he does not beget quickly, as we shall mention below.
So the saying in the chapter Ben Sorer is doubly anomalous. It was not stated as tradition,
nor because the truth of the verses compels us to take it as an event that actually happened in a just measure [lit. "in a just hin"; cf. Lev 19:36], but in the manner of "I am expounding Scripture," as we said.

When the discerning reader also turns to the books of the gentile sages, who speak in all simplicity, to see whether a male child is truly born to one so young,
he will surely find abundant testimony for everything we have said.
First, see what the chief of the Christian sages wrote in book 15, chapter 15 of his City [Augustine, De civitate Dei]. ⚠3
He raised the question whether one should judge that the early men, of whom it is written only that they begot at a hundred or two hundred years, had no offspring until that time, or not.
He answered that perhaps their maturity and their power to beget were delayed in their youth in proportion to the delay of the onset of their old age,
or that Scripture took care to mention only the son from whom the line descending to Noah and Abraham our father came forth.
You will find words like these also in Ramban at the end of Parashat Bereshit.
Since the matter has come before us, let us say that the statement of the master, the author of the Guide, in part II, chapter 47, is in truth more fitting in our eyes [than those who make all of them long-lived]. ⚠4
He wrote as follows: "I say that only that individual man lived those long lives, while the rest of mankind lived the natural span" etc.
Yet, begging pardon of his glorious eminence, the other view on this matter mentioned above is more in keeping with the Torah and more acceptable. ⚠5

Be that as it may! You see that the Christian's first answer, which we mentioned, suits our purpose.
Likewise in book 16 of the same work, chapter 11, resolving a difficulty he raised about the lifespans of Nimrod and Peleg, he wrote that those who beget early and often have short lives, and the reverse.
You too can lend him support from what you see in the plants of the earth: "which came up in a night and perished in a night" [Jonah 4:10].
And of the phoenix, which lives many days, they said in Bereshit Rabbah 19 that it is unique on earth.
In book 15, chapter 12, mocking those who say that one of our years was ten years for the ancients,
he said: "If so, Adam, who was a hundred and thirty years old when he begot Seth, was then thirteen,
and before that, when he begot Cain and Abel, he was only twelve.
And who could beget at such an age, according to the norm and law that nature is seen to follow?
If you say this was possible for Adam because he was created in his full stature and form,
what will you reply about his son, born to him like any other child, who was a hundred and five years old when he begot Enosh?
By your account he had not yet reached eleven. And all the more so Kenan, who was seventy when he begot Mahalalel: his age then would have been seven years. All this is vanity."
End of his words. That nature requires what we said, that whoever begets early dies early and the reverse,
see what the great writer Pliny wrote in book 7, chapter 2 of his Natural History, in the name of the philosopher Galitarco: ⚠6
that in one place at the ends of the earth there are women who give birth at seven years,
but on reaching thirty they are at the extreme of old age.
And in the name of Artemidorus he wrote that in one district of India the women give birth at five years, and do not live beyond eight. ⚠7

Solinus, an ancient writer, also mentioned this in his book of the wonders of the world, chapter 64. ⚠8
It is close to what we cited from the discussion in the first chapter of Yevamot, where they said of a girl of eleven years and one day, "lest she conceive" and "lest she die,"
since because of her youth there is but a step between her and death [cf. 1 Sam 20:3]. And the Philosopher [Aristotle], in the seventh book of the Politics, chapter 1, wrote that those concerned with the perfection of the offspring ⚠9
[hold that] the male should not marry until thirty-six and the female not until eighteen.
And in the fourth [book] On Animals, chapter 1, he wrote that the seed is not fully ready for generation until three times seven years, ⚠10
and that in the male it begins around the fourteenth year. For the understanding it is easy to know and verify the truth of all this
by considering the nature of the generative seed, which the Philosopher defined in the fourth book of the aforementioned On Animals as the final residue of nourishment.
Galen likewise, in his book of Medical Definitions, wrote in agreement with him.

The words of Ibn Sina [Avicenna, Canon], book 3, fen 20, treatise 1, chapter 2, are these:
"The seed is the residue of the fourth digestion, which takes place when the nourishment is distributed among the organs,
and it issues like sweat from the veins after the completion of the third digestion."
End of his words. So the adult, who needs nourishment only to replace what is dissolved,
may well have enough left over to beget, since nature prepares it for this abundantly.
But the child, who also needs to add to his growth,
surely lacks the natural capacity proportioned to him to prepare a residue for seed.
Accordingly, the more a person hastens to this before his time, the weaker in nature the offspring will be.

For this reason the sage and physician Fernel [Jean Fernel], foremost of the authorities of our generation, in the second part of his works, book 6, chapter 17, on the causes of barrenness and failure to beget, ⚠11
wrote these words on the reasoning of the great natural philosophers mentioned:
"Barren and unable to beget are children whose lower beard has not sprouted, and likewise the decrepit aged,
for their seed is thin, watery, and weak, unfit for generation."
End of quotation. The sage R. Isaac Latif, in the eighth [section] of his book Sha'ar ha-Shamayim, wrote as follows:
"The time when seed begins to issue from the male and the woman's flowers [menses] begin is usually after fourteen full years.
Then his hair sprouts in the private parts, and it is like the blossoms of a tree, which it begins to put forth before the fruit."
Shortly afterward he wrote: "The time for males to beget is after twenty years, for then the seed is complete,
and if he begets before that, the offspring will be weak and imperfect."
End of quotation. All this is close to the words of the investigator we cited. And if you inquire among the sages of the imperial [Roman] laws, you will find
how Modestinus, in his law entitled Arrogatio, concerning one who takes another's son as his own, an act of no effect unless the boy's hair has sprouted so that he may be called an adult,
explained that the days of the sprouting mentioned begin when he is fully fourteen years old and are completed when he is eighteen.
End of quotation. It is reasonable to judge that, this cause being founded on the pillars of nature,
our Sages, of blessed memory, also said on its account in Avot, "At eighteen, to the wedding canopy" [m. Avot 5:21], as we mentioned,
since before then a person is not prepared for generation as is needful.

And the saying of Rav Hisda in the first chapter of Kiddushin, "That I am superior to my colleagues is because I married at sixteen; had I married at fourteen, I would have said to Satan: an arrow in your eye!" [b. Kiddushin 29b–30a],
surely belongs here too: whoever couples early weakens the generative organs.
It is close to what Rambam, of blessed memory, mentioned in his introduction to the Order Tohorot, "and desire fails" [Eccl 12:5], even toward the most beautiful of women.
That is why he said "had I married" etc. But before fourteen nature has no preparation for raising seed,
and even afterward one does not beget quickly, just as with the tree of the field mentioned, time passes between blossom and seed.
Since we have learned that by saying the early generations begot early
we come to comfort them yet end up distressing them, with a leniency that leads to a stringency and a favor that is harmful to them,
let us say in truth that this [saying] in Ben Sorer is mere aggadah. It was not stated as received truth,
but in order to expound the verses in any way they can bear, for the purpose expressed in their dictum "Expound and receive reward."
I have already informed you that all the plain-sense commentators, of blessed memory, refused to allow that those verses bear the Sages' intended meaning mentioned above.

For further clarification, know that what the author of Seder Olam, chapter 2, maintained, saying "Haran matured at eight years and begot Iscah, that is Sarah,"
is rejected in the Ben Sorer passage itself, where it objects: "Perhaps it reckons them by their wisdom." See there.
I shall not refrain from saying that, for all we have written on this until now,
it is true that from what is written in 2 Kings 16, "Ahaz was twenty years old when he began to reign, and he reigned sixteen years in Jerusalem" [2 Kgs 16:2],
and afterward in chapter 18, and likewise in 2 Chronicles 28 and 29, where it is written of Hezekiah that he was twenty-five years old when he began to reign,
it follows that Ahaz begot him at eleven. Know, however, that while I have found none of the sages of Israel who says anything about this,
a certain Christian scholar named Tostado [Alonso Tostado], one of their great commentators on Scripture,
took note of the anomaly in his commentary on 2 Kings 16.
After laying down as an established premise that it is naturally impossible for a man to beget at eleven,
he said that others resolved this difficulty by saying one should believe that Hezekiah did not reign immediately upon his father's death,
but that for certain reasons the throne stood vacant, or he did not sit on it securely, for a number of years; and we add that number to Ahaz's eleven.

He, however, rejected this answer, saying that had it been so, it could not have gone unmentioned in either Kings or Chronicles.
On his own he answered that it is the way of Scripture with partial years sometimes to count them as full years and sometimes not to count them at all,
as Rashi, of blessed memory, also wrote in Kings on the verse "in the seventeenth year of Jehoshaphat" [1 Kgs 22:52]; we shall mention his words again below in chapter 35.
Thus it is possible that Ahaz's twenty years were twenty-one incomplete ones, and likewise the sixteen were seventeen.
Conversely, Hezekiah's twenty-five were only twenty-four complete years and two or three months more,
so that you arrive at three broken years to add to the eleven, which comes to more than thirteen.
This age, even according to the authorities of the imperial laws, admits of generation,
though it is almost beyond the course of nature. End of his words. In truth the first answer may also be correct,
besides which, in the case of Ahaz's sons it already appears that by way of providence, and as a sign, they were born to him, or to the prophet who spoke to him, at an age unusual for parents. ⚠12

In any case, whatever is strange to nature is a novelty,
and it has already been concluded in the chapter Ha-Shohet and the chapter Elu Overin that one does not learn from it.
I was glad that, after writing all this,
I found confirmation of our words in Sefer ha-Mahadurot of R. Isaiah the First [Isaiah di Trani]. On the Ben Sorer passage, concerning the Bathsheba episode mentioned, from which one learns that Scripture calls an eight-year-old a "man" and a seven-year-old a "woman," he wrote as follows:
"I have a difficulty from what we say below: 'And when Bezalel made the Tabernacle, was he thirteen years old?' as it is written, 'every man from his work' [Exod 36:4].
Evidently, even in the early generations one is called a 'man' only at thirteen.
It seems to me that, although we find they begot at eight, one is called a 'man' only at thirteen, because it was a miracle, and we do not learn from miracles.
That is why the House of Hillel said: We do not learn from the early generations,

for what we find, that they begot at eight, was a miracle." End of quotation. So he wrote what we wanted.
