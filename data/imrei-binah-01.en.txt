Thus says Azariah min ha-Adummim: In the introduction to this work I undertook to write, in its third part, chapters that would contain various studies which I consider worth making known.
So now, with God's help, I shall fulfil what I vowed. The reader will see that they are discourses that do not keep to their own kind,
for their sheaves turn on different axes and divide into many sparks.
Some of them are related in some way to the preceding part, Hadrat Zekenim,
and some have nothing to do with it; and they also differ from one another.
In any case, I have put them all together for the reason the Sages of blessed memory gave in the first chapter of [Bava] Batra regarding some of the prophecies of the Twelve [Minor Prophets] (even though their holiness preserves them): "since it is small, it might be lost" [b. Bava Batra 14b].
These words of ours, then, differing in their flavour, the nature of one not like the nature of the other,
will resemble a garden of various plantings, where whoever goes down into it chooses each time the flower or fruit he wants from it, according to his appetite and the desire of his eyes.

Since I considered that each of these chapters contains many sayings of the Sages that truly deserve reflection,
I have called this part, which is a work separate from the preceding translation, by a name suited to it for this reason:
Imrei Binah ["Words of Understanding"]. Now this kind of study, which, as stated, looks toward different ends,
is found abundantly among the scholars of the nations, and finds favour and good regard in the eyes of many learned people.
But since among some of our people it is something new, of which there is no frequent vision,
I thought it good in this first chapter to say something in praise of what is found in each of the two kinds of study,
namely, the uniform kind that keeps to its own species, and the varied kind that shoots one [arrow] here and one there.
I say that this question is in truth a twin sister of another question, namely:

Which is better for those who seek learning, to learn from many people or to take for themselves a fixed master?
And since one side of these must be preferable to the other, it necessarily appears difficult that we find the Sages of blessed memory praising both equally, as if they were carried together side by side.
For in the first chapter of Avot they say twice, with affection, "Make for yourself a master" [m. Avot 1:6, 1:16],
while in the fourth chapter Ben Zoma says: "Who is wise? He who learns from every person, as it is said: 'From all my teachers I have gained understanding'" [m. Avot 4:1; Ps 119:99].
The difficulty, however, becomes greater when one examines the other sayings they of blessed memory have on the subject.
For in the chapter Keitzad Me'abrin they said: "The Galileans, who did not learn from a single master, did not retain their learning" [b. Eruvin 53a];
and likewise R. Zeira, in the first chapter of Hullin, said mockingly: "Joseph bar Hiyya learns from the whole world."
Thus they praise making oneself a master, as stated; and if so, their saying "he who learns from every person" has another meaning,
as if to say that it directs us to apply ourselves to study with the quality of humility, which will indeed be of great help to us in it;
for when we recognize that one may chance to learn some point of wisdom even from someone of low standing, we will not draw back in pride and arrogance,
but will humble ourselves before him and acquire it; learning will come from every source.

And a man whom we know to be of this nature will be regarded by us as wise, and more worthy of receiving Torah from his mouth than someone of the opposite [nature].
For so are the words of R. Akiva in [the chapter] Ha-Ro'eh [b. Berakhot 63b], in Avot de-Rabbi Natan chapter 11, and in Bereshit Rabbah 1, on the verse "If you have done foolishly in lifting yourself up" [Prov 30:32]:
both in that he expounds, "If you have made yourself contemptible for the words of Torah, you will in the end be lifted up,"
and conversely, as in the incident of Levi bar Sisi there and in Yevamot 86: "Why were you made contemptible in the words of Torah? Because you lifted yourself up." ⚠1
And R. Hanina was not ashamed to say, in the first chapter of Ta'anit and in the chapter Ha-Golin: "I have learned much from my teachers, etc., and from my students more than from all of them" [b. Ta'anit 7a; b. Makkot 10a].
Rava too, in that passage of Kiddushin concerning [women] who were not handed over for intercourse, said: "Bar Ahina (who was his student) explained it to me: as it is written, 'When a man takes a wife and has intercourse with her'" [Deut 24:1].
Or we may resolve the apparent contradiction in the sayings of Avot in the manner explained in the first chapter of the tractate on idolatry, namely: "for learning [the tradition], from one master,"
"but for reasoning," many opinions are necessarily acquired from many people [cf. b. Avodah Zarah 19a].

Along these lines, then, returning to the former question, we may say that study that is constant and aims at a single purpose will undoubtedly be more useful in bringing us to one end
than study that varies like manna to many flavours; and so the rulers say: "No one has ever defeated me but a man of one craft."
Yet this varied [study] likewise has an advantage over the constant kind mentioned,
for if it is not as useful, it is surely more pleasant and agreeable, like foods whose variety adds to the enjoyment.
And perhaps this is one of the intentions of their saying, in the first chapter of [Avodah Zarah] and in the chapter Keitzad Me'abrin: "A man should not make his Torah into bundles," and Rava said: "The rabbis knew this and transgressed it" [b. Avodah Zarah 19a; b. Eruvin 54b];
for the pleasure compels them to pursue many branches of knowledge which they cannot retain together.
Thus indeed spoke Seneca the philosopher, who compared them to a stomach filled with varied foods that it cannot digest well and that do not become assimilated to the body,
as is found among the physicians: Galen in his commentary on the first [book] of [Hippocrates'] Acute Diseases,
and Ibn Sina, book 1, "ofen" 3. But in any case, this varied study we have mentioned, ⚠2
apart from the added pleasure in it, as stated, may boast of the other wonderful benefit that they of blessed memory mentioned in the aforementioned first chapter of [Avodah Zarah],
namely, that through it we acquire reasoning and judgement regarding every new subject brought before us.

For when our observations and mere incidental disclosures multiply,
even if we do not reach different goals, our eyes will surely be opened by them, and we will gain in sharpness and discernment in every matter requiring intellectual consideration.
And perhaps this too is what they of blessed memory intended in the chapter Ha-Ro'eh and the chapter Ba-Meh Ishah when they said: "A man should recite [his learning] even though he does not know what it says, and afterwards reason it out," and should meditate to the point of breaking [cf. b. Shabbat 63a].
For in whatever way we hold fast to the pursuit of wisdom, benefit may be hoped for.
Thus this way of study that does not keep to its own kind, which with the help of the Rock we shall tread in these chapters of ours, does not lack a desirable advantage,
since by its power we gain strength in reasoning; and, as we have set out, it is also by nature sweet to the soul.
You will find this praise of it if you seek it also among the foremost orators of the nations.
For Tully [Cicero], in the third [book] of his Oratory and in the fifth of his Letters, described the reading of varied narratives as pleasant to the ears,
and said that therefore histories, which contain many rises and falls and changes, delight, and their account draws one on.
Quintilian too, in book 9, chapter 1, said that it is the nature of things to delight in variety,
and in book 11, chapter 12, he wrote that varied study guards us against weariness and strengthens the soul.

Now this property, in my opinion, belongs to it because man possesses many faculties that alternate in their watches,
and, as the Kuzari says at the beginning of Part 3 on the pious man, each of them delights in what suits its nature.
From varied study no single faculty grows weary, and the many [faculties] delight with added vigour.
So this is undoubtedly one of the reasons why everyone whose Torah is his occupation rejoices in the study of the Babylonian [Talmud], since, as we say in the chapter Zeh Borer, it is "mixed" with Scripture, Mishnah, and Talmud [b. Sanhedrin 24a],
and on its axes turn natural, mathematical, and logical [matters]. Also, as the sage [of the] Kuzari said in Discourse 3, there is in it what surpasses the art of disputation,
so that there is in it abundant nourishment and delight for all.
And if R. Zeira, in the chapter Ha-Po'alim, when he went up to the Land of Israel, sat forty fasts so that it would be forgotten by him [b. Bava Metzia 85a],
he objected only to the excessive casuistry practised by the scholars of Babylonia,
so much so that on account of it they are called, in the chapter Zeh Borer, "injurers" (hovlim),
unlike the study of the scholars of the Land of Israel, who for their gentleness and composure are called "pleasantness" (no'am) [b. Sanhedrin 24a; cf. Zech 11:7].

In general, then, it emerges that in this as against that among the kinds of study, whether keeping to its kind or varied,
there is something good and pleasing to the soul. And though one may excel the other with respect to one of the ends,
both surely are good in giving the reader pleasure and benefit.
Now when God touched my heart to leave behind me as a blessing a book containing some useful matters,
all the more so since I am about sixty years old and deprived of male children who, with good deeds, would "raise up the name of the dead upon his inheritance" [cf. Ruth 4:5],
and I did not find myself so well supplied as to provide and sell one kind of "wheat of Minnith and pannag" [Ezek 27:17], grain and bread to satiety,
I said: let my soul beat out what it has gleaned among the ears of the legumes, handfuls if not sheaves [cf. Ruth 2:16],
and I will hand to others like me "a little balm and a little honey" [Gen 43:11] and parched grain [cf. Ruth 2:14],
that perhaps the reader may "suck and delight" [Isa 66:11] in them with some delight. I hope to the God of my salvation that this too will be a son to me,
which, as they of blessed memory said in the chapter Ha-Ishah, and in the Yerushalmi of Berakhot, chapter Hayah Kore, and of Shekalim chapter 2, will merit me to dwell in the tent of the Most High forever [cf. Ps 61:5],
and that my lips, as they of blessed memory said in the chapter Helek and in Bekhorot chapter Kol Pesulei, will move in the grave [cf. Song 7:10; b. Sanhedrin 90b].

Or, between much and little, I shall be like Ben Bevai of the Yerushalmi at the end of Pe'ah and in Shekalim chapter Elu Hen,
who merits good as one of those who keep the charge of the sanctuary, to supply the wicks, ⚠3
all the more since the aim of my heart is nothing but truth and righteousness.
And since errors may perhaps go forth from me in this work of mine, which would be loathsome both to me and to the reader,
I hereby inform every intelligent person who loves purity of heart that he will do what is very good in my eyes if he criticizes me and specifies my faults with sound reasons, so as to remove a stumbling block from the way of our people.
And if he is a man of generous spirit, he will not at the same time cease to judge me favourably, as befits all of us,
and may his reward be complete from our God [cf. Ruth 2:12].
