Behold, I am resolved in my heart — if my God in His lovingkindness goes before me — to inquire and search out, in the chapters of this section to which I have given the name "Days of Old" [Yemei Olam], concerning the total of years that we count from the Creation: what root of truth is found in it,
and whether there is any ground, by sound proofs, for adding to it or subtracting from it.
Now, since the count from the [creation of the] world to the building of the Second [Temple] is in part made clear in the words of the Torah and the Prophets in a manner that leaves no room for doubt,
and in part is not made as clear in them as is needed — for which reason many distinguished later commentators on them have written opinions on it that do not agree with what is found among our Rabbis, as will be explained, with God's help, in chapter 35 and the chapters from there on —
just as with regard to the part that is clear we have nothing whatever to innovate,
so too with regard to the doubtful part just mentioned we have no essential additions to make, but only to recall and briefly clarify the words of those scholars who preceded us.
Yet together with the commentators mentioned, of blessed memory, who are held to be guardians of the Torah and from whose waters we continually drink,
we shall not refrain from also adducing the words of two ancient scholars from the time of the Second Temple,
who likewise were of our people and came "from the waters of Judah" [Isa 48:1], though they are unknown to most of those among us.

And since this is so — that in this inquiry we merely adduce the words of others and do not invent anything new of our own devising —
any measure that it may occur to someone to mete out in protest against those who have entered this fray
will doubtless rightly come back into their own bosom. You, then, discerning reader, shall hear from me where their disputes lie,
and what I find possible to say in defense of each of the parties, and in particular in defense of our Rabbis;
for in any case the count from the Creation that is customary among us — even if the one who would increase it adds, or the one who would diminish it subtracts —
shall never depart from our mouth, and the custom of the fathers shall remain Torah as in days of old.
Now the apology of the dissenters mentioned, and also the apology of any dissenter whose hand will appear the lower to men of inquiry,
and the fact that this investigation is in all its aspects far from opposing, God forbid, anything pertaining to the laws of the Torah, or from casting any blemish on the honor of our Rabbis, may they rest in peace —
have already been set out in the preceding chapters, and, with God's help, the "sun of righteousness" will shine on it again in those to come;
and in particular, before we leave this section, the matter will be clarified and made plain that the method our Rabbis, of blessed memory, adopted in counting the years
was a glorious wisdom, whose worth every intelligent person will recognize and make known.

Indeed, I foresee from the outset that you will say in your heart, pleasant reader: Is not this inquiry like "a law for the days of the Messiah" [hilkheta li-meshiha], and even inferior to it?
For what have we to do with it? What happened, happened, thousands of years ago or seven times as many! But you yourself will answer your own words: consider first how truth itself, which thousands of scholars have also sought in inquiries more remote than this,
is like the seal of God, Truth — the treasure of the fair and good soul, which all long for.
And second to it, and more weighty: in the course of this inquiry you will see that we shall have occasion to understand the reasons of some matters in the Holy Scriptures.
And you already know how, in the fourth [chapter] of Zevahim, on the matter of piggul in something done outside [the Temple], and in chapter 4 of ⟨Yevamot?⟩ regarding the daughter of a priest who committed harlotry, and in the first [chapter] of Yoma, on the question of how Moses clothed Aaron in his garments, ⚠1 ⚠2
they, of blessed memory, concluded that even regarding "a law for the days of the Messiah," which at a time like this has no relevance whatever for us,
we ought to investigate and reflect, whether to understand the verses or on the principle of "expound and receive reward."
And as will be further explained, if the Lord delights in us, when we speak of the priestly garments in chapter 46 of the fourth section.

And this is a third reason for this inquiry, and not an empty one:
for out of the debate there will be clarified a true "law for the days of the Messiah," which in the opinion of many distinguished persons is near to come now.
And to remove a stumbling block from the path of our people, it seems to us good and useful to speak of it today, as will appear, with God's help, before the close of this section, in chapter 43.
On account of these three in particular, apart from what will be further explained in the course of the discussion, I judge
that this subject is not only to be considered permissible and to be opened up, but also a religious duty, for which one may hope for reward from the lovingkindness of our God.
Now it is very clear, according to what was set out at length in two of our chapters,
that in secular matters [milei de-alma] — like this investigation in particular, on which, as we have already said, the observance of no law or commandment depends —
even any writer from among the nations of the earth may fittingly be trusted, all the more so one who speaks in innocence [mesiah lefi tumo] and has no stake in the matter.

Therefore, when there comes to our hand, concerning the account of some of the chronicles of the past pertaining to this inquiry, the testimony of various writers from among the scholars of each nation in its own language,
who, to make themselves a name among the great, investigated, refined, and recorded them, proceeding in their course with no aim other than to make them known to mankind as they were,
it may be thought that the intelligent person may have the voice of their testimony proclaimed in the camp of narratives, and found upon them a resting place for the sole of our foot;
and while also considering, as regards a religious duty, to uphold with all one's might the words of the holy dead who are yet alive,
he will not despise the sense of the words of the said writers, for he will see that there is found in them something of fine form for our subject.
However, to distinguish between them and the Sages, of blessed memory, to whose superior rank in our esteem it is fitting that we show deference,
we shall not consent to nor heed their words if they come by themselves alone,
until we have first been brought to some aspect of what is written in our holy texts;
for then their language too ought to be taken into account — "and he shall be like a chief in Judah" [Zech 9:7] — and further, [see] the verse [discussed] in Kiddushin chapter 1, ⚠3
all the more so since, from the one against the other, there may emerge an indication from which we can take [help] to clarify and make plain some doubt that arises for us in the words of the prophets of truth and righteousness.

Josephus [ha-Yosifon] has already written in his second book Against Apion that it is a great sign of the truth of what is narrated ⚠4
when we see that writers differing and separate, each in his own language and nation, agree and concur together.
The Philosopher, too, in the seventh [book] of the Ethics said something close to this: ⚠5
that the report which is heard among diverse multitudes does not entirely perish.
And on account of these things — although the foundation of this entire inquiry,
as will become evident to every reader, rests on the mountains of the Holy Scriptures and the words of our prophets —
before I begin to enter "the thick bosses of its shields" [Job 15:26], I gathered for myself a band of truthful chroniclers, among whom are also two of the ancient Hebrews who spoke the Greek language in their books,
which is perhaps the reason why they received no mention among our Rabbis in Babylonia;
and they are the ones of whom I said beforehand that their rank is distinct. Their number in all is five, whose words, whether little or much, will find a place in the inquiry;
and they are ancients from the time of the Temple or close to its destruction, not "new ones who came lately" [Deut 32:17].

These are their generations: Xenophon the Greek, who, according to Eusebius of Caesarea in his chronological tables, ⚠6
lived in the days of Artaxerxes Mnemon, who for them is the eleventh of the kings of Persia, as will be further explained below;
and Metasthenes the Persian, who lived in the days of Seleucus the Greek, called by the Sages Seleucus [Silikos],
one of the four kings who arose in place of Alexander; and Yedidyah the Alexandrian [Philo], of Jewish descent, whose excellence we made known earlier, and who pleaded on their [the Jews'] behalf before the Second Destruction in the presence of the emperor Gaius;
and Josephus the Jew, "the man who has seen" [Lam 3:1] his affliction; and Eusebius the Christian, a man of Caesarea, who lived in the days of the emperor Constantine.
All of them are men of good name among writers, and you shall hear how they speak fittingly to what we have sought.
And as for the results and conclusions that will emerge from their words: any essential statement in which we see them disagreeing among themselves, or disputing a single letter of the Holy Scriptures,
shall be trodden underfoot, and we shall not set our heart to it. But that on which they agree, and against which no proof from the prophetic books comes to contradict it,
or where there is found in one of them enough to rouse our ear to decide some doubt that arises for us in understanding them [the Scriptures],
you, wise-hearted reader, place your palm upon it; for although we do not found [anything] upon them,
is there not in their account "a sign, which is something" [simana milta hi], as we mentioned from the outset?

And it is already known that many of our commentators — such as the author of the Arukh, s.v. sheva',
Rabbenu Saadiah, Ibn Ezra, Nahmanides, Kimhi, and Don Isaac [Abravanel] — especially concerning what pertains to the Second Temple in the prophecies of Haggai, Zechariah and Malachi, and in Daniel and Ezra,
adduce support for their words from what is found in Josephus and other writers who are not of the children of Israel.
Therefore we shall not by rights be despised by men of understanding,
even if, with regard to these [writers], our right hand pushes away while the left draws near [b. Sotah 47a],
all the more so as all is for the sake of truth and for the sake of Heaven.
Now, as is publicly established in steadfast faith, the part of the total of years from the Creation that is known through the Torah which Moses set before us,
as well as what is explicitly stated in the tradition of the prophets who came after him, who wrote the chronicles as a memorial in a book,
does not fall under any investigation or inquiry, for "just and upright is He" [Deut 32:4], and "what is the man that can come after the King" [Eccl 2:12], our God?

But the part in which the spirit of the Lord did not stir, which only individual scholars of the generations relate and set out for us,
not as they saw but as they heard from the ancients of the earth —
apart from all their apology which has already been set out and which will yet be stated below —
it is known that through the vicissitudes of the times and the turmoil of evils and their changes, some error and confusion of order may have crept into it;
and therefore those who understand it will not labor in vain over it, and the refiner and purifier will sit over it, in the expectation that his fellow will likewise come and search him out, as happens in every human study and investigation,
until God will be pleased that "the earth shall be full of knowledge" [Isa 11:9] and truth shall not be doubtful or lacking.
Is it not known, as we wrote long ago, that if part of the total counted in the years of the Creation according to our Rabbis were by its nature transmitted to them by word of mouth from Sinai like all the other matters of tradition,
there is no stranger among us, and he who sins wrongs his own soul who would open his mouth over it in vain;
for by right and in righteousness all flesh among our people would come to bow to them and to their words, "bowing his head like a bulrush" [Isa 58:5],
and cursed be he who does not uphold them or devises iniquity against them.

But since, as for the number of years written in the Torah and the Prophets, behold, "none of the men of might have found their hands" [Ps 76:6] to count them except as any man of those who have "understanding of the times" [1 Chr 12:33],
for the holy spirit had already ceased in the Second Temple, and thus R. Simeon b. Gamaliel complained in Bereshit Rabbah 37 that even in his days they did not make use of it —
then, though we consent, as is fitting for us, to accord of the glory of our God to those who fear Him, and to say that if all the sages of the nations of the world were in one pan of the scales and one of them [our Sages] in the other, he would outweigh them all,
this gate shall not be closed, and "the righteous shall enter it" [Ps 118:20] to see whether there is found in the prophetic books something that poses a difficulty for what was received by them and from them;
for then it is a duty upon us to seek and search out the explanation of the matter in an upright way and not with empty fancies [buki seriki], until we see that "righteousness and truth have met" [cf. Ps 85:11].
But let us return to the presentation of the words of the five scholars we have mentioned.
Let your eyes, discerning reader, look straight ahead: since what is extant of the said Metasthenes and Yedidyah [Philo] consists of short chapters, I have not refrained from copying them as they are,
so that even some details which will be of no concern to you here
you may perhaps put to use for another matter in the rest of the book, or in something else that may occur to you.

But for the other three writers it sufficed me to take their grapes, the grapes of the inquiry, and to press them into the abridgment that seemed fitting to me.
