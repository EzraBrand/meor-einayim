Among the accounts on which our Sages differ from Yedidyah the Alexandrian and from Josephus [who wrote] for the Romans, both of whom the Christian scholars follow, ⚠1
there is the following: [our Sages] of blessed memory say that Simeon the Righteous was high priest in the days of Alexander of Macedon, and that it was his likeness that appeared [to Alexander] as the victor [in his battles].
This can be seen in the Jerusalem Talmud, Shekalim; Megillat Ta'anit, chapter 9; [b.] Yoma, chapter 1; the beginning of chapter Terof [Qalpi]; Menahot, chapter 13; the end of [Va-yikra] Rabbah on Shemini; and other places besides.
And they make this Simeon, who was one of the last of the Men of the Great Assembly, the second generation after Joshua son of Jehozadak, the [first] high priest of the Second Temple.
For in their view all the Men of the Great Assembly lived together in a single generation,
as is also evident from their statement in Bereshit Rabbah, in the section on the rainbow: "the generation of Hezekiah and the generation of the Men of the Great Assembly."
And in Midrash Tehillim on Psalm 36, on the verse "Continue Your steadfast love to those who know You" [Ps 36:11], they said: "Two generations made use of the Explicit Name: the generation of the Men of the Great Assembly and the generation of the persecution." The same may be learned from the work called Seder Tannaim va-Amoraim, which, as appears from its contents, was composed in the year 4647 of Creation,
for it states as follows: "Haggai, Zechariah and Malachi transmitted it to Ezra, and Ezra to the Men of the Great Assembly, who are Zerubbabel, Joshua, Nehemiah, etc., and the Men of the Great Assembly [transmitted it] to Simeon the Righteous."

In my opinion all the later authorities followed it, such as Rabbenu Samson of Chinon in Sefer Keritut at the beginning of the section Yemot Olam,
Rabad [Abraham ibn Daud] at the beginning of his Sefer ha-Qabbalah, Maimonides in his introductions to the Commentary on the Mishnah and to the Mishneh Torah, Bertinoro at the beginning of his commentary on tractate Avot, and many others,
on the basis of arguments that, God willing, we shall yet explain.
And on the belief that these two generations, those of Joshua and of Simeon, followed directly one upon the other,
our Rabbis in Seder Olam, chapter 30, and in tractate Avodah Zarah, chapter 1, accepted the view of R. Yose that the Persian kingdom, which was cut off by Alexander of Macedon, lasted only thirty-four years in the time of the [Second] Temple;
and when these are added to the period of the Greek kingdom, the Hasmonean dynasty and the Herodian dynasty, the total comes to 420 years, which is the duration of the Temple.
The other party we mentioned, however, places Simeon the Righteous in the eighth generation from Joshua,
for in their view there were between the two the six high priests whom Nehemiah mentions in chapter 12, saying: "And Jeshua begot Joiakim" etc., up to "begot Jaddua" [Neh 12:10–11].
And they hold by tradition that Jaddua begot Onias and Onias begot Simeon the Righteous, eighth in the generations from Joshua; and they say that Alexander lived in the days of Jaddua, the sixth generation mentioned, and that it was to him that he bowed down and him that he honored in all the things that [our Sages] of blessed memory attributed to Simeon.

Therefore this party lengthens the duration of the Persian kingdom,
and with it they lengthen the days of the Temple far beyond what our Rabbis said.
Now this general matter is not the purpose of the present chapter, for, God willing, we shall go through all its details in its proper place in these chapters.
Rather, our purpose here is that, after this remark we have written on the difference between Jaddua and Simeon the Righteous,
I shall inform you that Rabad ha-Levi in the aforementioned book, knowingly or unknowingly, confused [separate] matters,
and the learned Don Isaac [Abravanel] too, who followed after him, kneaded in his company a sorry dough to which Nehemiah the governor bore witness.
For the aforementioned Rabad, at the beginning of his aforementioned book, wrote as follows:
"The second generation of the Men of the Great Assembly: Simeon the Righteous, whose name was Ezra son of Joshua son of Jehozadak." Thus far [his words].
Likewise R. Isaac Israeli, author of Yesod Olam, chapter 18 of Book 4, took it from him and wrote: "Simeon the Righteous is Iddo son of Joshua son of Jehozadak."

And the learned Don Isaac, who also relied on him, wrote at the beginning of [his commentary on] the book of Haggai as follows:
"The first high priest of the Second Temple was Joshua son of Jehozadak, and after Joshua his son Iddo served, as written in the book of Ezra." Thus far [his words].
And in his introduction to tractate Avot he wrote as follows: "Simeon the Righteous was high priest, and he was the son of Joshua son of Jozadak the aforementioned high priest, who was the brother of Ezra the Scribe." Thus far [his words].
And in the commentary on the mishnah of Simeon the Righteous he wrote there as follows: "He was also called Hananiah, according to the words of Yosippon." Thus far [his words].
But if you search the entire book of Ezra and Nehemiah you will not find any Iddo as high priest;
only Jaddua is mentioned, whom Yedidyah the Alexandrian in his book On Times, and Josephus [writing] for the Romans, together with the rest of the aforementioned party, call
in Greek and Latin "Iaddus." Likewise in the Hebrew Yosippon, in the German printing and the two early printings of Abraham Conat,
when he mentions the episode of Alexander with the high priest, you will find only "the high priest" without any proper name;
only near the matter of Manasseh, who set up the altar on Mount Gerizim, he wrote as follows:

"Manasseh, brother of Iddo the high priest who was in Jerusalem." Thus far [his words].
For Iddo was high priest then — that is, not Iddo but Jaddua.
And I believe that the name Iddo is not the word of Yosippon himself, who would have written "Jaddua,"
but rather [that of] some corrupter who saw that all the scholars of the aforementioned party [wrote] "Iaddus,"
[and] believed that in our language this would be called "Iddo," and put it into the book.
Rabad and Don Isaac also found a Turkish version, from which the Venice printing was copied,
for when it mentions the episode of Alexander with the high priest it says "Hananiah" or "Honio,"
and near the aforementioned matter of the altar it calls him "Iddo"; and so the matters became confused for them in the places mentioned, and they combined them like two horses pulling in different directions, neither according to the words of the Sages of Israel nor according to the words of the other party, and neither this nor that was upheld by them,
as the author of Halikhot Olam says in the last chapter concerning [the rule] "the halakhah follows the one who decides between them."

For it is in no way to be believed that our Sages refused to call him by the name Jaddua, well known to him from Holy Scripture,
or even, if you will, by the name Hananiah or Iddo, according to whichever of the versions of the Hebrew Yosippon, and gave him a new name, Simeon the Righteous, for no known reason, so that the well-known [Jaddua] became unknown — quite apart from the essential difference that would need examining regarding the duration of the generations, whether they were two, or six, or eight, as we mentioned.
Now since I saw that the learned Don Isaac, in his commentary on the aforementioned mishnah of Simeon the Righteous, [wrote] that Aristotle, the teacher of Alexander of Macedon, wrote in one of his letters
how he spoke with this Simeon in Jerusalem and found him in divine matters full of the blessing of the Lord,
and how in many of his letters he also wrote on particular matters, "this is among what the Simeonite answered me,"
it seemed right to me to call your attention to something I know. It is that R. Joseph ibn Shem Tov, in the introduction to his commentary on the Ethics, exaggerated even more than the aforementioned Don Isaac, for he said that he saw in Egypt a book in which was written how Aristotle at the end of his days admitted, without shame, everything written in the book of the Torah of Moses and became a righteous proselyte;
and likewise, if my memory does not deceive me, something similar is found in the book Magen Avraham by the learned Farissol.
And when my soul roamed to know from where they had this, I did indeed find in Diogenes Laertius, who wrote the life of this philosopher, and likewise in Caelius Rhodiginus, book 2, chapter 2,
that Aristotle at the end of his days fled his place to the city of Chalcis,
because they had denounced him for having despised the worship of the idol in female form, Ceres Eleusina, of great renown and greatly feared in the land of his people, Athens,
in that he, Aristotle, who had fallen in love with a concubine of his friend Hermias, offered to her the sacrifice reserved by their chief priests for the aforementioned goddess. ⚠2
And he became a true sign, like King Solomon in Israel, [of the truth] in the first speech of Zerubbabel before Darius, that nothing is as strong as a woman, who even for the wise of heart is snares and her heart nets [cf. Eccl 7:26].
But as for his coming to the true religion, as they say, I have neither heard it nor find it reasonable.

And if it is an error on their part, I believe it arose from things they heard which Josephus found in his first book Against Apion,
which Eusebius of Caesarea also cited in book 9 of his Praeparatio [evangelica], chapter 3,
and among the moderns Pico, lord of Mirandola, book 2, chapter 2, of the book on the study of philosophy; ⚠3
and the learned ibn Bibago, author of Derekh Emunah, also brought them, with many errors, in Gate 3, folio 46.
But whoever reported to them that he was a Jew on the strength of that account, by my judgment lied and knew nothing of the matter;
or else they, if they read [it], did not review it nor study deeply the place I mentioned —
for the words written there on this matter are lengthy indeed.
But their substance is only that Aristotle told his companion Clearchus how on one of the Greek islands he happened to associate for many days with a great sage who spoke Greek, and judged him to be a Jew because he had come from the lands of the Jews to find him there,
and that in truth he brought him more wisdom than he, Aristotle, gave him. Thus far.

For Josephus mentioned all this in order to stuff back down his throat the lies of Apion, who said that the Jews are a lost nation, a people without name or remembrance among the great men of former generations;
on the contrary, he cites there the words of many ancients, men of stature, who mention us with praise, and he brings this philosopher too among them, as we have related.
Go and see, if your heart prompts you to consult the place of those words, and you will find this account of mine faithful before you;
and if you examine well, you will discern whence the error came to the one who said that Aristotle became a Jew,
for the description "Jew" written there, for one who punctuates the words correctly, refers to that Jew and not to Aristotle.
But let us return to the matter of the learned Don Isaac. I say that since I have asked all those who have descended into the great sea of Aristotle's books, and no one reports to me any mention of a Simeonite in any of them,
it is quite possible that whatever whisper of the matter he heard came to him in some way from the aforementioned account.
And granted that that Jew existed and his name was Simeon — do we not [learn] in [b. Bava Metzia] chapter 1 and Gittin, chapter Kol ha-Get, that even in a small town with few people we take into account [the possibility of] two [men named] Joseph son of Simeon? All the more so in the whole breadth of the earth: go out and see how many Simeons who are not "the Righteous" are in the marketplace!
And in particular you should consider the philosopher's statement that that Jew was recognizable to him as a Greek by his speech, as we mentioned.

Now concerning Simeon the Righteous, with whom we began this chapter, you should know that on any view a difficulty appears in the words of [the Sages] of blessed memory.
For it is known that he was one of the last of the Great Assembly, many years before the translation made in the days of his brother Eleazar and Ptolemy Philadelphus, the third of the Greek kings from Alexander. Yet in Megillat Ta'anit, chapter 11; the Jerusalem Talmud, Sotah, chapter Eglah; our Gemara, chapter Elu Ne'emarin; Tosefta Sotah, chapter 13; and the Midrash on Song of Songs on the verse "If she be a wall" [Song 8:9], it is stated that Simeon the Righteous heard a voice issuing from the Holy of Holies saying: "Gaius Caligula has been killed and his decrees annulled."
And this Caligula, at the end of Seder Olam, chapter 30, is counted last of eight Greek kings,
for thus it is written there: "These are the kings of Greece: Alexander of Macedon,
Pirațos, Țalkon, Seleucus, Sanțiros, Anțiyon, Antiochus, Caligula," who apparently was at the beginning of the reign of the Hasmoneans, who fought against the Greeks, as is known. ⚠4
Nor can one say that all of these reigned together at the beginning of the Greek kingdom, for he counted among them Alexander, who was unique in his generation, and after him his kingdom was divided into four, according to Daniel's well-known prophecy.
The resolution of this, in my opinion, is that there were two [men called] Simeon the Righteous.
See, in the Book of Times of Yedidyah the Alexandrian, Simeon the Righteous II son of Onias II, both of whom were descendants of the first Onias and Simeon the Righteous.

Accordingly it is possible that the name Caligula is a corruption, and the correct [reading] is Callinicus; for in the tables of Eusebius of Caesarea and of ha-Samoțiyo you will find that opposite Seleucus Callinicus, who was about seventy years into the Greek kingdom, they mentioned another Simeon the Righteous who was in his time. ⚠5
Also at the end of the book of Ben Sira — which you know our Sages rejected only with one hand —
among the many prophets, sages and mighty men whose praises he extols at length, he mentions this Simeon the Righteous son of Onias, who was to Israel like a shining light, brought them deliverances, and greatly increased the glory of our God.
And Ben Sira, the author of the book, lived in the time of that Simeon, as the aforementioned Eusebius wrote in his tables; and these were certainly after the aforementioned translation.
I said "Ben Sira the author of the book" to exclude Ben Sira his grandson, who published this work of his grandfather, as appears from his preface there.
Now the existence of two bearers of this glorious name has a good parallel, namely Rabbenu ha-Qadosh,
for you know their statement in Kiddushin, chapter 4, and in Midrash Kohelet on the verse "The sun rises" [Eccl 1:5]: on the day R. Akiva died, Rabbi, also called Rabbenu ha-Qadosh, was born;
yet in the Mekhilta on Be-shallah, the section of the Song, you find: "R. Akiva said: Rabbenu ha-Qadosh told me something" etc. — so here are two "holy ones";
though I shall not keep silent that it is not unlikely to be an error in the Mekhilta, for in Shemot Rabbah 24 the aforementioned saying is cited with the reading "R. Abba told before Rabbenu."
Or we may say that the correct [reading] is Caligula as written, and this Simeon is Simeon son of Mattathias, the high priest.

This is also implied by the fact that in our Gemara, the aforementioned chapter Elu Ne'emarin, and likewise in Tosefta Sotah, chapter 13, it is taught: "It happened with Yohanan the high priest" etc., and then: "It happened with Simeon the Righteous" etc.;
whereas had this Simeon been the earlier one, of the last of the Great Assembly, they would have placed him before Yohanan in order.
Indeed I shall not deny that in the Jerusalem Talmud, end of Sotah, Simeon is placed before Yohanan in these deeds of theirs. [As for] their statement in Megillah, chapter 1, [on] "I will not reject them" [Lev 26:44] etc.: "in the days of the Greeks, when I raised up for them Simeon the Righteous and Mattathias son of Yohanan the high priest" etc.,
it refers to this Simeon the Righteous, who earned renown more than his brothers Judah and Jonathan, sons of the aforementioned Mattathias,
for all the mighty hand that he showed in Israel, as you will find in First Maccabees, chapters 14 and 15, so that they set up in his memory on Mount Zion a bronze tablet on which all his deeds were engraved, as we shall mention again in chapter 25.
And he lived after the two aforementioned Simeons, at the end of the Greek kingdom, which with his sword and bow he cut off from our land.
